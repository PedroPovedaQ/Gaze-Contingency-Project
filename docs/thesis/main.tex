\begin{abstract}
Speech has long been studied as a way to interact with and direct people through three-dimensional space, from Bolt's \emph{Put-That-There}, which combined spoken commands with pointing~\cite{bolt1980putthatthere}, to early comparisons of spoken route directions with maps~\cite{streeter1985tell}. Advances in virtual and augmented reality have renewed this line of work. Augmented reality systems combine spoken and spatial cues to direct attention toward objects~\cite{zhang2023seehear,binetti2021outofview} and ground spoken instructions in the user's live view~\cite{kim2026speechtospatial}. In virtual reality, on-demand spoken directions help users complete the final, precise stage of reaching a target~\cite{tanaka2026coarse}. The same technology could improve quality of life for blind and low-vision people, for whom spoken spatial guidance can make locating objects more independent~\cite{coughlan2020spatial,tanaka2026coarse}.

How guidance is spoken also matters. Listeners recognize personality in synthesized speech and respond more favorably to synthetic voices whose personality resembles their own~\cite{nass2001personality}. Work on virtual health assistants has examined how vocal delivery shapes user engagement~\cite{maxim2025modulation}. The user's own voice has also emerged as a persuasive medium: AI-generated encouragement in one's own voice was perceived as uniquely engaging and personal~\cite{fang2025emotional}. Stronger evidence comes from an online programming game, where players assigned a self-similar avatar voice completed more puzzles, played longer, and reported greater immersion than players assigned a dissimilar voice~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar}. In virtual collaboration, self-similar voices increased a virtual character's likability and believability~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}.

This thesis asks whether the object-search experience changes when gaze-contingent guidance is spoken in a self-similar voice rather than in a neutral voice the participant has chosen. It examines three research questions: whether voice self-similarity impacts performance and perceived workload (NASA-TLX); eye-tracking indicators of workload (IPA) and of visual search efficiency (fixations and saccades); and engagement in the object search task. The central hypothesis is that a self-similar voice makes guidance more engaging, and that this engagement improves search.

Follow My Voice investigates this comparison in a within-participant virtual-reality user study. Participants complete one practice trial in their first assigned voice and two counterbalanced blocks of 40 measured trials. Seated participants search for colored geometric objects arranged on eight vertical planes around them in a virtual environment, including locations initially outside their field of view. Initially, participants are not informed of which object they should find. Instead, an agent that uses the participant's gaze data as context guides them toward it with spatial hints such as ``turn to the left'' or ``look up.'' Participants select the target by pointing the right controller at the object and pressing the trigger. Both trial conditions use the same hint guidance to help participants find the object. Condition-specific questionnaires are administered after both blocks.
\end{abstract}

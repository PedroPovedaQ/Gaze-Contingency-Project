\begin{abstract}
Directing users' attention to objects of interest has long been a concern in virtual reality (VR), particularly when relevant objects lie outside the user's current field of view~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,lange2020hivefive}. Beyond visual cues, researchers have investigated audio alerts for visual targets in VR~\cite{sabouni2025guidance}, spatialized sound for out-of-view targets in augmented reality (AR)~\cite{binetti2021outofview}, and spoken hints for AR object search~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. Spoken assistance can connect spatial instructions with objects in the surrounding environment, guiding users toward a region and then the intended object. Such guidance also has accessibility applications: verbal directions have helped blind users locate points of interest on physical objects~\cite{coughlan2020spatial}. The experience of receiving assistance can also be influenced by the tone and voice identity of spoken instructions. Research on virtual collaboration has found that self-similar voices can increase likability and believability but can also elicit greater eeriness~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}. As interactive agents take on a more active role in guiding users through virtual environments, the experience of receiving their assistance becomes an important design consideration. These findings motivate examining how self-similarity affects users when the voice serves as a spatial guide. This thesis compares gaze-contingent guidance spoken in a synthetic cloned voice resembling the participant's own (self-similar) with guidance spoken in a neutral voice selected by the participant from a supplied catalog.

Follow My Voice investigates this comparison in a within-participant virtual-reality user study. Participants complete one practice trial in their first assigned voice and two counterbalanced blocks of 40 measured trials. Seated participants search for colored geometric objects arranged on eight vertical planes around them in a virtual environment, including locations initially outside their field of view. Initially, participants are not informed of which object they should find. Instead, an agent that uses the participant's gaze data as context guides them toward it with spatial hints such as ``turn to the left'' or ``look up.'' Participants select the target by pointing the right controller at the object and pressing the trigger. Both trial conditions use the same hint guidance to help participants find the object.

The study compares performance in terms of active search-completion time, alongside perceived workload, motivation, and agent experience. Condition-specific questionnaires are administered after both blocks.
\end{abstract}

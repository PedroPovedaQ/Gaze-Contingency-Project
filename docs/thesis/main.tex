\begin{abstract}
Voice has long been studied as a way to interact with and describe three-dimensional spaces, starting from the seminal work \emph{Put-That-There}, published in 1980~\cite{bolt1980putthatthere}. Recent advances in virtual and augmented reality have reignited interest in voice as an interface for interacting with these new modalities. Previous research has shown that cues grounded in 3D space can help users locate and understand objects in that space~\cite{zhang2023seehear,binetti2021outofview,kim2026speechtospatial,tanaka2026coarse}. In terms of accessibility, audio-based guidance systems that use spoken and spatial audio as the interface have been researched for blind and low-vision individuals~\cite{coughlan2020spatial,tanaka2026coarse}.

How guidance is spoken also matters. Interestingly, listeners recognize personality in synthesized speech and respond more favorably to synthetic voices whose perceived personality resembles their own~\cite{nass2001personality}. Going further, the user's own synthetically cloned voice has also been researched as a persuasive medium, and AI-generated encouragement in one's own voice has been perceived as uniquely engaging and personal~\cite{fang2025emotional}. In virtual agentic collaboration, self-similar voices increased a virtual character's likability and believability~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}.

The aim of this thesis is to study how voice impacts agentic instruction in an object-search scenario where the research subject can only find the object using audio-based hints, and where the voice conditions are a self-similar voice resembling the research subject and a preferred agent voice of the subject's choosing. The study is performed within-subjects in two counterbalanced blocks. Seated participants search for colored geometric objects arranged randomly on walls surrounding them in 360 degrees, which includes objects initially outside their field of view. An agent, which is gaze-aware and knows the location of the object of interest, guides the user towards it with grounded spatial hints such as ``turn to the left'' or ``look up.'' Performance is measured by search time, perceived workload by NASA-TLX, and visual search by eye-tracking measures.
\end{abstract}

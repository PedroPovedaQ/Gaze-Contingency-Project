\section{Participants}
\label{sec:participants}
Eligible participants are consenting adults able to understand the task, hear the guidance, view the scene with usual correction and safely use the seated setup and right controller. Visual correction and prior XR and voice-assistant experience are recorded. Sample size was planned by simulating the 96-trial design and fitting the planned mixed-effects model to each simulated dataset, with even participant counts from 12 to 60 and with assumed trial-level noise, individual differences in the response to voice, timeouts, incomplete trials, and dropout (Figure~\ref{fig:power-curves}). The study will begin by enrolling 20 participants, 10 in each voice order, which is what is feasible for this thesis. Under the middle assumptions, this gives about 74\% power to detect a 15\% difference in search time between the voices and about 39\% power to detect a 10\% difference. The corresponding values are nearly 100\% and 93\% under the optimistic assumptions, and 26\% and 13\% under the pessimistic ones. Reaching 80\% power for a 10\% reduction would require about 52 participants under the middle assumptions. Added trials help only modestly: at 20 participants, the 10\% power under the middle assumptions is 39\% with 96 trials and 35\% with 64, because individual differences in the response to voice are reduced only by adding participants. For the paired NASA-TLX and IMI interest/enjoyment comparisons, about 17 usable pairs reach 80\% power only at a large standardized effect, about 0.8 after Holm adjustment~\cite{holm1979simple}, and these scores do not benefit from more trials. The study is therefore sensitive mainly to large effects: a nonsignificant result will not show that voice has no effect, and results will be reported as effect estimates with confidence intervals. If enrollment is extended, the decision will be made without inspecting the voice comparison. These values are planning estimates. The variance inputs are assumptions rather than measurements, because the development recordings contain no paired voice data, and they will be revisited after pilot sessions.

\section{Experiment Design}
\label{ch:methodology}
The user study uses a within-participant design to answer the research questions in Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}.

The study design draws on Chiossi et al.'s visual-search study, which combines search outcomes with eye-tracking measures~\cite{chiossi2024searching}. This study keeps that separation between task performance and visual behavior but changes the research question to voice identity, initially withholds the target identity, and uses a researcher-selected 25-second search limit. Complementary work by Pfeil et al. at UCF compares eye--head coordination in virtual and physical viewing~\cite{pfeil2018eyehead}. That work motivates retaining head motion alongside eye direction when interpreting gaze in this application.

\subsection{Voice Manipulation and Comparison Rationale}
This thesis compares two ways of personalizing assistance: a synthetic voice derived from the participant's recorded speech and a Selected Agent Voice chosen by that participant from the supplied catalog. Letting participants choose this alternative ties the comparison to a design choice a user could face. The within-participant task provides repeated observations under both configurations. Similarity ratings indicate whether participants heard the clone as resembling themselves, whereas performance and experience measures compare how each voice functions as guidance.

Gaze contingency and the agent's decision rules are common to both conditions. Because the target identity is initially hidden, participants receive repeated guidance in the assigned voice on every trial. The decision model, memory, and gaze awareness are held constant rather than varied as independent factors.

\subsection{Design and Trial Allocation}
The study uses a within-participant comparison of two voice conditions: a Selected Agent Voice and a self-similar synthetic voice. Both conditions use the same guidance policy, semantic phrase library, selection method and visual-feedback settings, although actual phrase sequences may differ because guidance responds to behavior. Because the Selected Agent Voice is chosen by the participant whereas the clone is derived from the participant's speech, the two voices may also differ in preference, gender presentation and synthesis quality. The self-similar clips also receive the inner-voice audio treatment described in Section~\ref{sec:voices}, whereas Selected Agent Voice clips do not. The contrast therefore cannot isolate self-similarity from these features.

The researcher assigns each participant to Order A (self-similar first) or Order B (Selected Agent Voice first) from a roster kept outside the application, 10 per order; the application records the assignment but does not randomize it or enforce the quota. Counterbalancing the order distributes possible carryover from the first block across the two voices~\cite{williams1949residual}, although it does not remove carryover. Each participant completes one practice trial followed by two measured blocks of 48 trials, one per voice. The practice trial uses the first voice in the assigned order (the self-similar voice for Order A, or the Selected Agent Voice for Order B) and is excluded from measured outcomes. A seed makes the trial schedule reproducible. Nominal horizontal-angle quotas are identical across blocks, with trial order and left/right assignments randomized subject to those quotas. Within each block, each bilateral angle category (45, 90 and 135 degrees) has six left and six right assignments. Each block is divided into six shuffled groups of eight trials, each of which contains every signed plane step. Each of the 24 shape-and-color identities appears twice in every block.

Nominal angle steps are defined relative to the readiness (start) object plane, which is the previous target plane. For the first measured trial, this reference is the practice target plane. The first measured cross is centered on its object plane. Each later cross is shifted sideways by a seeded offset of up to 20 percent of the usable half-width. Target placement happens when readiness completes, while all objects remain hidden and before the opening cue. At that moment, the application rotates the horizontal bearing from the participant's eye origin to the cross by the assigned signed angle, places the target where that bearing meets the assigned object plane, and arranges the remaining objects around it. The exact starting angle defined below therefore equals the assigned category at that instant. If the required position falls outside the object plane, the session stops so the participant can recenter, rather than substituting a different angle or trial. Final positions depend on the saved eye origin as well as the seed, so each layout is reconstructed from the saved object positions.

\subsection{Starting Angle and Measures}
\label{sec:theta-measures}
Each trial is characterized by its starting angle $\theta_0$, the horizontal angle between the direction of the readiness cross and the direction of the target, as seen from the participant's eye position. Let $\mathbf{o}$ be the eye origin saved when readiness completes, $\mathbf{c}$ the readiness-cross center, and $\mathbf{p}$ the target center, all in the same world coordinate frame. With $\Pi(\mathbf{v})=(v_x,0,v_z)$ projecting a vector onto the horizontal plane, define
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}=\Pi(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{o}),\qquad
\mathbf{b}=\Pi(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{o}).
\end{equation}
The starting angle is then
\begin{equation}
\theta_0=\operatorname{atan2}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\cdot(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}),\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}\right)\frac{180}{\pi}.
\end{equation}
Its sign gives the direction of the turn, and its magnitude $|\theta_0|$ gives the horizontal turn demand of the trial (Figure~\ref{fig:starting-geometry}). Each block schedules six trials at each of the eight signed plane steps, which gives six trials each at $0$ and $180$ degrees and twelve each at $45$, $90$, and $135$ degrees, and the target is placed so that the realized angle matches the assigned one. Because a larger angle requires a larger turn, the analysis adjusts for $|\theta_0|$ rather than dividing completion time by degrees. Appendix~\ref{app:measurement-definitions} gives the remaining details of the snapshot and the height and distance measures.

\subsection{Research Questions and Outcomes}
Each research question in Section~\ref{sec:research-questions} maps onto specific outcomes. RQ1 uses active completion time as the primary outcome, with missed targets and perceived workload, measured with an adaptation of NASA-TLX~\cite{hart1988nasatlx}, as secondary outcomes. RQ2 uses the Index of Pupillary Activity~\cite{duchowski2018ipa}, fixation count, and saccade frequency, which remain exploratory until the eye-event detectors are validated on the headset. RQ3 uses the interest/enjoyment subscale of the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (IMI)~\cite{ryan1982control,mcauley1989imi}, with the other three IMI subscales reported alongside it. Agent perception, eeriness, voice experience, reliance, search behavior, and variation in the voice contrast with starting angle are exploratory. The hypotheses are directional, favoring the self-similar voice, but the voice contrasts are tested two-sided so that an advantage for the Selected Agent Voice would also be detected. Repeated trials are treated as within-participant observations, not as independent participants.


\section{Procedure}
Participants perform an agent-guided object-search task while seated on a swivel chair, with multiple colored geometric objects scattered across eight surrounding vertical planes. Although the application knows the target identity, the participant does not receive a color-and-shape description before searching. Instead, the agent guides the participant toward the target plane and then refines the direction horizontally, vertically or diagonally. The participant selects an object using the right-controller ray and trigger. Looking at an object, receiving a gaze confirmation or pointing at it does not by itself complete the trial.

Each trial begins with the participant looking at a readiness cross. Once readiness is established, an opening directional cue plays while the objects remain hidden, and object reveal then starts active search timing. A correct trigger selection before the 25-second active-search deadline stops timing and produces success feedback. An incorrect selection is counted, and search continues within that same limit.

If the target has not been selected after 25 seconds of active search, the trial ends as a timeout. The application hides the objects, cancels pending spatial hints, and plays a prepared encouragement in the assigned voice, beginning with ``Time up,'' before presenting the next readiness cross. For example, the agent may say, ``Time up. Let's shake that round off and start the next.'' Separate wording introduces the block break or acknowledges the final trial. The same deadline and phrase set apply to both voice conditions and to the single practice trial. A timeout advances the scheduled attempt without adding a replacement. Explicit pauses suspend the clock, whereas tracking loss alone does not. The 25-second limit is a researcher-selected protocol parameter, not a threshold drawn from the cited studies.

Before participant use, consent materials will be updated to describe ElevenLabs transcription, cloning, and synthesis, including the data transmitted, retention, access, and deletion arrangements. After consent, participants audition the catalog voices and choose their Selected Agent Voice; this voice is chosen explicitly rather than inferred from participant gender. They then complete an automatic audio intake survey. A generic intake agent, whose voice is outside the catalog and is never assigned to either condition, reads nine fixed prompts: four eligibility questions (age, hearing, vision with usual correction, and ability to stay seated, turn in a swivel chair, and use the right controller), four background questions (VR, voice-assistant, and eye-tracking experience, and current visual correction), and one prompt asking the participant to talk a friend through finding a lost object for about thirty seconds. The open-ended prompts provide more natural speech for cloning, and the last one is close to how the agent speaks. A tone marks when each answer should begin; recording then starts and stops automatically and the intake moves to the next prompt, while the researcher can pause, repeat, skip, or stop at any time. All answers are transcribed with ElevenLabs and stored as local participant data under the study identifier. ElevenLabs is the only outside service that receives participant audio or transcripts; decisions during the task are made by the local model. Voice capture begins only after a signed-consent attestation is recorded. Once all nine prompts, and the optional fallback reading when the answers are too short, are complete and the answers reach at least 30 seconds of estimated speech and 50 transcribed words, the system clones the participant's voice from the answers and begins preparing both voices' phrase libraries in the Trial Review application under the participant's study ID. The researcher takes no action to start this step, and the audio therefore reaches ElevenLabs before the eligibility review. While the libraries are prepared, the researcher reviews the four eligibility answers. A negative or unclear answer stops screening rather than passing automatically; library preparation then stops and the clone is deleted from ElevenLabs, and withdrawal at any point has the same effect. Only a positive review and a complete phrase library permit transfer to the headset, where the participant confirms their study ID and condition order before starting. In Trial Review, the researcher follows six steps: reserve the study ID, attest consent, choose the Selected Agent Voice, run the audio intake, review eligibility, and send the session to the headset. The next unused study ID is proposed automatically and can be edited, with a warning for duplicates, and rehearsal sessions use a separate range so that they do not consume study IDs. The intake questions and the fallback reading passage, used only when the answers do not reach that threshold, are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:instruments}.

There is no separate headset screen for choosing the clone, because it is prepared from the participant's own answers.

The researcher fits the headset and conducts vendor calibration. Interpupillary distance is not entered by hand. During the headset voice checks, the application passively records two separate estimates: the distance between the left and right rendered views, read from the headset runtime, and the distance between the left and right eye origins, read from the eye tracker. Each estimate is the median of at least 40 stable readings spanning at least 2 seconds, and is stored with its source, time, and sample count. The headset does not report whether its automatic IPD adjustment ran, so the application records only whether the view separation was observed to change during capture, which cannot establish whether an adjustment took place. A missing or unstable reading produces a warning without stopping the session, and the researcher can then enter a value, which is recorded as manual. These are engineering settings, the two estimates may not be independent, and neither is a clinical measurement of IPD; their agreement with the headset's lens setting will be checked during pilot sessions. Within the application, gaze preparation then records three calibration dots, each preceded by a 3--2--1 countdown, which fit a provisional head-relative yaw and pitch correction. Five separate verification dots follow automatically and test that correction without changing it (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration-steps}). The researcher can then continue, retry, or skip without a correction, which remains a pilot setting. Standardized instructions explain that participants rely on the agent to discover the target and use the right trigger to select it. Participants then complete the single practice trial and the two assigned 48-trial blocks.

No questionnaire interrupts the block sequence. Instead, the final survey presents every condition-specific item once, with two response columns side by side, labeled \emph{Selected Voice} and \emph{Self-Similar Voice}, so that participants compare the two agents directly while rating each one. A break is offered at the block boundary, and the researcher can pause at any point, which is logged immediately and stops the search clock. Tracking is rechecked between blocks and after headset adjustment. After headset removal, participants complete the side-by-side retrospective ratings, comparative voice preference, and debrief. Participants may request a pause or stop, and symptoms or technical problems prompt researcher assessment.

The survey columns name the two voices directly, as the Selected Voice and the Self-Similar Voice, so participants do not need to remember which block used which voice. Because the label identifies the self-similar condition, it may cue expectations about that voice; this tradeoff is noted as a limitation, and pilot sessions will check that participants understand both labels.

\subsection{Pilot Testing and Parameter Checks}
\label{sec:pilot}
Before confirmatory recruitment, pilot sessions verify the realized angle/height schedules, target visibility and seated comfort, readiness and selection behavior, gaze validity, audio timing and wording, and reconstruction of trial events from the saved data. Pilot assessment also measures survey comprehension and completion time. These checks inform calibration-acceptance, practice-proficiency and technical-stop criteria. The pilot also assesses whether the 25-second deadline is suitable, and the outcome is documented with the study build and questionnaire versions.

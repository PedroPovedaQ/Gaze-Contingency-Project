\chapter{RESULTS}
\label{ch:evaluation}
This chapter is organized around the three research questions introduced in Section~\ref{sec:research-questions} and closes with a discussion of how the possible outcomes would be interpreted. Each section states what will be reported for its question and how that evidence tests the corresponding hypothesis. Participant data have not yet been collected, so no voice-condition results are reported here, and each figure shows simulated data in place of results; the development evidence that supports the apparatus appears in Appendix~\ref{app:development}.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis-plan}
Because every participant hears both voices, each research question is analyzed within participants. For RQ1, the primary outcome is active completion time, analyzed with a trial-level mixed-effects model of log completion time that includes voice condition, period, assigned voice order, trial position within the block, and the absolute pre-cue starting angle $|\theta_0|$; participant random effects are chosen from the design rather than from statistical significance~\cite{barr2013random}. The primary result is the voice contrast, reported as a time ratio standardized over a common distribution of starting angles and tested two-sided, so that an advantage for either voice can be detected. Missed targets and NASA-TLX workload~\cite{hart1988nasatlx} are secondary outcomes. For RQ2, IPA, fixation counts, and saccade rates are compared in the same within-participant way once the eye-event detectors are validated. For RQ3, the IMI interest/enjoyment subscale~\cite{mcauley1989imi} measures engagement, with the other IMI subscales reported alongside it. Questionnaire scores are treated as one observation per participant and voice block rather than as trial-level data. Secondary comparisons are grouped into prespecified families and adjusted with Holm's procedure~\cite{holm1979simple}, while agent-perception and custom items are exploratory and reported with effect estimates and uncertainty. Searches that reach the 25-second deadline have no completion time and are excluded from this model, which pulls its estimate toward no effect if one voice also reduces timeouts. A complementary completion-probability or time-to-event analysis will therefore be specified before confirmatory analysis.

\section{RQ1: Performance and Perceived Workload}
RQ1 asks whether voice self-similarity impacts performance and perceived workload. The primary result is the adjusted contrast in active search-completion time. Its estimand is the marginal time contrast standardized over a common starting-difficulty distribution, and here \emph{standardized} refers to that common distribution rather than to a standardized effect size. The estimate is reported with its uncertainty, the eligible participant and trial counts, and model diagnostics, following the analysis plan finalized before confirmatory recruitment. Missed targets, incorrect trigger selections, and valid gaze and controller contact latencies are reported by voice condition alongside paired participant summaries. Perceived workload is reported from the six NASA-TLX dimensions and their unweighted mean~\cite{hart1988nasatlx} at the participant-by-block level. Together, these results test H1: that the SSV improves search performance and lowers perceived workload. Figure~\ref{fig:results-rq1-time} presents completion time, and Figure~\ref{fig:results-rq1-workload} presents missed targets and workload.

\section{RQ3: Engagement in the Search Task}
RQ3 asks whether voice self-similarity impacts engagement in the object-search task. Engagement is reported from the interest/enjoyment subscale of the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory~\cite{mcauley1989imi}, with the effort/importance, pressure/tension, and value/usefulness subscales reported separately, using multiplicity families defined in the analysis plan. This result tests H3: that the SSV makes the search more engaging, and Figure~\ref{fig:results-rq3} shows it alongside the other subscales. Read together with RQ1, it also shows whether higher engagement and better performance occur in the same condition, as the thesis's central hypothesis proposes; because both outcomes follow voice assignment, their co-occurrence does not by itself establish that engagement causes the performance difference.

\section{Guidance Delivery Across Voice Conditions}
A fair voice comparison depends on both voices receiving comparable guidance. Reporting therefore describes gaze availability, actual speech exposure, delayed acknowledgments, interruptions, and unhelpful cues by voice condition and session. This account helps assess whether technical delivery problems affected one condition more than the other. The voice contrast is not automatically adjusted for behavior-dependent hint counts or waiting time. These quantities arise after voice assignment, may themselves depend on voice condition, and cannot be treated as baseline difficulty. \section{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}
\subsection{Interpreting the Research Questions}
Follow My Voice asks whether a synthetic voice resembling the listener changes how people perform a task or experience it, compared with an SAV the listener has already chosen, using object search as the task. Participant results are still pending, so the interpretations below are conditional. They describe what the data could support, not which voice is better.

The discussion follows the three research questions. RQ1 concerns measured search performance and perceived workload, RQ2 concerns eye-tracking indicators of workload and search efficiency, and RQ3 concerns engagement. We read these outcomes together. Agreement among them, in either direction, would give a consistent picture of how the voice changes the work, whereas disagreement could reveal a tradeoff that a single outcome would conceal.

\subsubsection{RQ1: Performance and Perceived Workload}
A completion-time advantage for the SSV would have to be read alongside the uncertainty of the estimate, the manipulation check, and guidance quality. If the SAV condition showed systematic intelligibility or delivery problems, the difference could reflect those problems rather than anything specific to self-similarity. If both conditions have comparable delivery quality and participants recognize the clone, the result would more directly support a voice-condition effect in this task. Even then, resemblance would remain confounded with other differences between the chosen and synthesized voices.

Faster completion with the SAV would be consistent with a chosen voice being easier to follow, but also with intelligibility or delivery problems in the cloned condition. Delivery records and acoustic-quality reporting could help distinguish these readings, although the study could not establish the mechanism without further evidence. Liking, naturalness, and reliance ratings may help characterize the experience in either case. A correlation between such ratings and performance, however, would not establish that they mediate the voice effect.

If completion times are similar, the width of the uncertainty interval becomes central. A wide interval would leave practically important differences unresolved, whereas a narrow interval near the null could constrain the size of a plausible effect under the tested conditions. A nonsignificant test does not establish equivalence. The analysis plan includes no equivalence test, which would require a meaningful bound and procedure specified before the result is examined.

Perceived workload is read alongside completion time. Lower NASA-TLX workload with the SSV would fit H1, and higher workload would run against it. Faster search with unchanged or higher workload, under either voice, would suggest that participants worked harder rather than that the guidance was easier to follow.

\subsubsection{RQ2: Eye-Tracking Indicators}
If the eye-event detectors are validated, fewer fixations and saccades and lower pupillary activity with the SSV would be consistent with H2. The opposite pattern would suggest less efficient visual search under that voice, and similar values would show no detected difference. These indicators depend on coverage, so a difference must be read against the eligible time in each condition; unequal coverage alone can produce different counts, as the development recordings illustrate (Section~\ref{sec:development-eye-measures}). If the detectors cannot be validated, RQ2 remains unanswered rather than being answered with interaction proxies such as ray hits.

\subsubsection{RQ3: Engagement}
Efficiency and experience may also diverge. A preference for self-similar speech may occur without a detectable improvement in completion time, while efficient search may coexist with discomfort. Diel and Lewis attribute vocal uncanniness to deviation from typical organic voices and report that synthetic voices escaped the uncanny valley~\cite{diel2024uncanny}, so discomfort with a clone may depend on how far it departs from familiar voices rather than on synthesis itself.

Prior studies place a self-similar voice in different roles. Kao et al. examine an avatar voice in a setting where continued voluntary play can reflect engagement, whereas Guo et al. report favorable impressions alongside eeriness in virtual collaboration~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar,guo2024doppelganger}. Aymerich-Franch et al. examined one's own voice while giving a speech through an avatar~\cite{aymerich2012voicesimilarity}. In Follow My Voice, the voice instead instructs the listener through a fixed search task. Faster completion therefore addresses efficiency, while enjoyment, discomfort, and debrief responses address the experience of following it.

If the SSV is rated more engaging, the result would fit H3. If the SAV is rated more engaging, or the ratings are similar, it would not. Because both outcomes follow voice assignment, engagement and search time moving together would not show that engagement causes a performance difference.

\subsection{Practical Relevance and Transfer to Other Tasks}
Object search was chosen because it is a common task in VR, and the question behind the study is broader than search: whether a self-similar voice changes how people work with an agent. The results themselves, however, apply to the tested task. Spoken guidance could be useful for locating task-relevant items in maintenance or training, finding a book, or noticing relevant VR content~\cite{binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear,lange2020hivefive}, but this experiment does not test a voice in those settings. The present study addresses a voice-choice question. It does not test industrial task completion, learning transfer, navigation safety, or narrative comprehension.

The practical value of the study depends on how its outcomes combine. Faster search accompanied by acceptable workload and a comfortable voice experience would support considering self-similar guidance for comparable tasks. A favorable experience without a detected speed difference could still motivate offering the clone as an optional voice, without supporting a claim that it makes search more efficient. Slower search, discomfort, intelligibility problems, or a preference for the SAV would instead mark limits to that option.

Transfer also depends on how much information users already have. In Follow My Voice, the participant must use spatial guidance to infer an initially unnamed target, whereas in Zhang et al.'s book task the target title was supplied~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. A known-target task may allow people to continue independently and encounter the voice less often. Real work can additionally require recognizing meaningful parts, remembering procedural steps, moving through space, or coordinating with another person. Replication in those contexts would need to establish both the quality of guidance and the effect of voice choice.

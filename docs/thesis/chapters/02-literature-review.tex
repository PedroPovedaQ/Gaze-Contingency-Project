\section{The Voice of an Agent}
Speech has been studied as a way to interact with objects in space since Bolt's seminal work \emph{Put-That-There}, which combined spoken commands with pointing to help users both place and move objects in a spatial display~\cite{bolt1980putthatthere}, and advances in virtual and augmented reality have renewed this area of research~\cite{zhang2023seehear,kim2026speechtospatial,tanaka2026coarse}. Recent research indicates that the voice delivering an instruction can influence how users experience an interaction as well as how they engage with a task. Previous work has shown that listeners recognize personality in synthesized speech and respond more favorably to synthetic voices whose personality resembles their own~\cite{nass2001personality}. Venkatakrishnan et al. studied an agent that asked clarifying questions during task guidance, measuring task duration, NASA-TLX workload, and perceptions of the agent~\cite{venkatakrishnan2026clarifying}. We follow the same logic here, treating how well a person works with an agent and how that work feels as separate questions.

\section{Self-Similar Voice}
Instant voice-cloning methods have made it practical to synthesize a voice resembling a specific speaker~\cite{qin2023openvoice}, and the user's own self-similar voice has been studied for its possible behavioral effect: AI-generated encouragement in one's own voice was perceived as uniquely engaging and personalized~\cite{fang2025emotional}. There is also some evidence that a self-similar voice can change what people do. Kao et al. compared self-similar and assigned dissimilar avatar voices in an online programming game with 657 participants, and the self-similar voice led to more puzzles completed and longer voluntary play, although the reported intrinsic-motivation measures were not significant~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar}. Aymerich-Franch et al. found that hearing one's own voice, rather than another participant's, while giving a speech through an avatar in VR increased self-presence and social presence~\cite{aymerich2012voicesimilarity}. This demonstrates at least some preference for a self-similar voice.

Another pioneering study showed that a self-similar voice made a virtual collaborator more likable and believable, though also more eerie~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}. Rosi et al., who also tested listeners' own voices, found that familiar listeners rated clones the same as or lower than recordings on trustworthiness, attractiveness, and competence~\cite{mcgettigan2025cloned}.. In Follow My Voice, self-similar synthesis creates a cloned voice resembling the participant, allowing the study to examine whether that resemblance changes the experience or effectiveness of receiving instructions from an agent, in terms of performance and perceived workload, eye-tracking indicators of workload and search efficiency, and engagement in the task (Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}).

\section{Object Search as a Task in Virtual Reality}
Visual search is an everyday task~\cite{kelley2025cueing}, and object search in virtual and augmented reality has been studied extensively in head-mounted displays~\cite{gruenefeld2017eyesee360,trepkowski2019fov,chiossi2024searching,quinn2024review,kelley2025cueing}. This has utility in augmented reality applications such as maintenance, repair, and training~\cite{binetti2021outofview}. It is a useful setting for our question because it has established measures, and because researchers have already investigated a variety of methods to point individuals toward objects in virtual and augmented reality; from visual~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,gruenefeld2017eyesee360} to auditory or audio-tactile~\cite{marquardt2020guidance,tanaka2026coarse}, or a combination of both~\cite{binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear}.

When an object begins outside the field of view, assistance must first support attention orientation, directing the user toward a relevant region of the scene. Once that region becomes visible, the user still needs to distinguish the intended object from nearby distractors.

Audio-based methods specifically could improve quality of life for blind and low-vision people. Spoken and spatial audio guidance has helped blind users find locations on objects and acquire targets, which may support independence~\cite{coughlan2020spatial,tanaka2026coarse}.

Research on auditory guidance also supports using sound to help users locate objects. In head-mounted AR, Binetti et al. combined visual arrows with spatialized alarm sounds to guide users toward out-of-view targets~\cite{binetti2021outofview}. In a VR simulation of narrow field-of-view AR, Marquardt et al. found audio-tactile guidance slower than a comparable visualization technique but equally accurate, with users exhibiting better situation awareness~\cite{marquardt2020guidance}. In addition, Zhang et al.'s AR book-search study used audio and visual hints to help identify a specific book on a shelf~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. In VR, Tanaka et al. supplemented continuous spatial audio with on-demand spoken directions such as ``up'' while blind participants reached for targets; this verbal mode produced the lowest error rates, fastest completion, lowest workload, and strongest preference~\cite{tanaka2026coarse}. We take heavy inspiration from this type of vocal guidance in our research.

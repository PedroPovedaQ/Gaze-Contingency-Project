\chapter{LITERATURE REVIEW}
\label{ch:literature}
AR and VR are being investigated across an expanding range of spatial tasks, from finding objects in outdoor environments to receiving instructions tied to a surrounding scene. This review first situates the study among these applications, then examines voice identity and self-similarity, attention guidance, and the gaze-based interaction that supports the present comparison.

\section{AR and VR Applications for Spatial Assistance}
Kumaran et al. compare compass, radar, and in-world arrow aids during outdoor AR search for virtual objects among physical and virtual scene content~\cite{kumaran2023navigation}. At a different scale, Yang et al.'s AR navigation preprint combines building information with an embodied guide and directional arrows to assist navigation queries~\cite{yang2025embodiednavigation}.

Other work addresses spatial instructions within a task rather than movement toward a distant destination. Binetti et al. investigate locating out-of-view objects in head-mounted AR, while Zhang et al. study visual and auditory hints during AR search~\cite{binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear}. VR offers a complementary setting in which the arrangement of targets, distractors, and initial viewing directions can be controlled and recorded. Chiossi et al.'s comparison of search across physical and virtual content further connects object search with measurements of efficiency and attention~\cite{chiossi2024searching}.

Follow My Voice concentrates on the voice delivering spatial instructions: it compares self-similar and neutral speech within a common VR search application, rather than comparing AR with VR or replacing a navigation interface.

\section{Voice as a Social and Personalized Interface}
Research on machine-generated speech has considered personality, source attribution, and social presence~\cite{nass1993voices,nass2001personality,lee2005socialpresence}. These perspectives motivate treating voice as more than a neutral carrier of words.

In this project, personalization takes two forms. In the neutral condition, the participant chooses among existing voices in the supplied catalog; in the self-similar condition, recorded speech is used to create a synthetic voice resembling that participant. Both forms may produce familiarity or liking, but through different routes: preference is expressed directly when the neutral voice is chosen, whereas resemblance is introduced by synthesis and must be confirmed through participants' perceptions. For this reason, the manipulation is better described as a comparison between two forms of personalization than as personalized versus generic voice.

Research on customizing voice-assistant personality has also examined similarity between assistant and user~\cite{snyder2023onevoice}. Personality similarity, however, is conceptually different from acoustic self-resemblance: a listener can prefer a speaking style without hearing it as their own voice, and a close acoustic resemblance can be experienced as uncomfortable. The planned manipulation check therefore asks about perceived voice similarity, while the experience battery separately asks about liking, naturalness, familiarity, and uncanniness.

Voice choice has also been studied experimentally. In a programming game, Kao et al. independently varied whether participants could choose their avatar's visual appearance and voice~\cite{kao2022audiomatters}. Voice choice increased avatar identification and perceived autonomy when visual choice was also available, but did not produce those benefits when visual appearance was assigned. This conditional effect is relevant to the participant-selected neutral voice used here.

\section{Self-Similar Voice and Embodied Collaboration}
Two particularly relevant studies examine resemblance in different roles: an avatar speaking as the player and a virtual collaborator speaking to the participant. The present study extends this question to a guiding voice without a visible agent body.

Kao et al. analyzed 657 participants in an online programming game, comparing self-similar and assigned dissimilar avatar voices while also manipulating pitch~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar}. Self-similar voice was associated with more puzzles completed, longer voluntary play, and higher similarity identification, competence, relatedness, and immersion. Direct differences on the reported intrinsic-motivation subscales were not significant.

The meaning of performance also differs across tasks. In Kao et al.'s game, longer voluntary play can indicate continued engagement, whereas in the present fixed search task a longer trial can indicate difficulty. Follow My Voice interprets completion time alongside workload, motivation, and reported reliance, without treating speed as a complete account of the value of self-similar voice.

Guo et al. used a 25-participant, within-subject design crossing appearance and voice self-similarity during virtual jigsaw collaboration~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}. Self-similar voices increased likability and believability ratings while also increasing eeriness. Voice did not significantly affect co-presence or perceived intelligence, although appearance affected those measures.

Both studies compared self-similar voices with assigned alternatives, whereas Follow My Voice compares the clone with a neutral voice selected by the participant~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar,guo2024doppelganger}. The question is therefore whether resemblance to oneself offers a different experience from a voice one has already chosen.

The present study also requires a distinction between the embodiment of the participant and that of the agent. Participants wear a headset and use a tracked controller, so their interaction involves physical sensing and action, but the guiding agent is not represented by a co-present animated character. Measures developed for an embodied collaborator are therefore reported as adaptations.

Applications of self-voice to daily reminders and emotional self-voice interventions extend this research beyond games and virtual collaboration~\cite{kim2024selfvoice,fang2025emotional}. They motivate asking whether participants can imagine uses for their own synthetic voice. The debrief therefore begins with open questions before introducing probes about eeriness, self-talk, and possible applications.

\section{Naturalness, Familiarity, and Uncanniness}
Voice resemblance is not the only acoustic difference that listeners may notice. Synthesis can change articulation, prosody, loudness, duration, or perceived naturalness. Research on vocal uncanniness motivates measuring these qualities separately rather than subsuming them under similarity~\cite{diel2024uncanny}.

Both conditions use the same semantic library and speech provider. This controls content but not acoustics, because prepared clips can differ in duration and pronunciation even when their text matches. Pilot checks assess intelligibility and comfortable playback levels for neutral and cloned voices, and the recorded provider, model, voice, and library identifiers document the configuration each participant heard.

Custom experience items and the appealing, eerie, and familiar items selected from Guo et al. are analyzed separately, because no measurement model combining them has been established for this study. A participant may report a voice as both familiar and eerie, and preserving the individual item directions keeps that response interpretable.

\section{Workload, Motivation, and Agent Perception}
Task speed describes efficiency, whereas workload, motivation, and agent perception address different aspects of receiving help.

Zagermann et al. discuss eye tracking as a means of investigating cognitive load in visual computing~\cite{zagermann2016workload}. This work motivates considering gaze-derived measures, but an unvalidated count of ray hits is not a workload measure. The confirmatory plan therefore relies on subjective workload dimensions and task performance; pupil, fixation, and saccade analyses remain contingent on separate acquisition and event validation.

Nerella et al. investigate gaze-based cognitive-load prediction during an AR color-matching task with interrupting alerts, using 31 participants and Magic Leap 2 eye tracking at 60 Hz~\cite{nerella2026gaze}. Their group XGBoost model achieves a reported test AUC-ROC of 0.85. Positive labels are tied to participants' reports of overload in response to alerts, and the authors report no significant correlation between high-load report frequency and NASA-TLX. The study supports investigating gaze alongside subjective workload while preserving the distinction between momentary reports and retrospective ratings.

NASA-TLX supplies six workload dimensions~\cite{hart1988nasatlx}; this study adapts it with a 0--10 response range and an unweighted complete mean. The Intrinsic Motivation Inventory distinguishes interest/enjoyment from related subscales such as effort, pressure, and value~\cite{ryan1982control,mcauley1989imi,csdtIMI}, and the subscales are reported separately. The instrument appendix gives the item wording and reverse keys needed to reproduce scoring.

The agent-perception measures are taken from Guo et al., who attribute them to their original sources~\cite{guo2024doppelganger,biocca2001networked,moussawi2019perception}.


\section{Visual Search and Attention Guidance in Extended Reality}
To study a guiding voice, the task must give its instructions a meaningful role. Object search provides that role by requiring the participant to connect guidance with a location and then an intended object.

Search in a surrounding display differs from search on a single front-facing screen because relevant objects may lie outside the visible field. Turning changes what can be inspected, while eye movements refine attention within the visible region. Work on eye and head movements across an extended field of view is therefore relevant to the task geometry~\cite{stein2024eyehead}. In the present study, this motivates recording both head motion and gaze, since neither fully substitutes for the other.

Several approaches to directing attention in immersive environments inform this design. Grogorick et al. address subtle gaze guidance, while HiveFive concerns immersion-preserving attention guidance~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,lange2020hivefive}. Ariza and colleagues investigate vibrotactile assistance and proximity-based multimodal feedback for selection~\cite{ariza2017vibrotactile,ariza2018proximity}.

A recent paper, RippleVision, provides a visual counterpart to gaze-contingent spoken guidance. Kudnick et al. describe brightness ripples restricted to a cone between a point of interest and the current gaze location, with opposite brightness modulation in the two eyes~\cite{kudnick2026ripplevision}. This summary draws only on the published abstract; its detailed methods and comparative outcomes are not assessed here. The technique illustrates how assistance can adapt to a participant's gaze without identifying the target verbally. Follow My Voice holds the assistance policy constant across voice conditions and does not compare guidance modalities or assume that visual and spoken cues have equivalent effects.

Binetti et al. examine visual and auditory cues for locating out-of-view objects in head-mounted augmented reality~\cite{binetti2021outofview}, and Warden et al. focus on target cue type and location in augmented-reality search~\cite{warden2022visual}. Follow My Voice does not open with a color-and-shape description of the target; instead, the sequence of directions gradually narrows the participant's search.

The hidden-target design also distinguishes this study from known-target search. A participant who already knows the target can continue searching without further assistance, whereas here the participant must obtain enough spatial information to infer which object is intended. The voice may therefore be heard more often, and may matter more, than in a task where it supplements an already known target.

\section{Eye Gaze and the Choice of Interaction Modalities}
\subsection{Gaze as Context for Assistance}
Eye tracking provides an observation of where the user is looking that can be compared with the target's position. This makes it useful for adapting guidance as a search unfolds: a broad turn instruction may be relevant while the target is outside the current view, whereas a small vertical correction may be more useful once the user reaches its region.

Existing gaze-contingent auditory techniques provide precedents for this adaptation. Losing et al. vary a tone with gaze-to-target distance, and Kwok et al. combine initial orientation with finer guidance~\cite{losing2014guiding,kwok2022twostep}. Their desktop studies support investigating feedback tied to ongoing gaze. EmBARDiment offers a more recent XR example of using gaze context to support an assistant's interaction~\cite{bovo2025embardiment}. The present system applies this rationale to short spatial instructions while keeping the guidance policy common across voices.

\subsection{Speech, Visual Feedback, and Deliberate Selection}
The modalities have complementary roles. Gaze supplies context, speech communicates a direction, visual feedback helps users understand interaction state, and the right-controller trigger provides an explicit selection. Keeping selection separate from looking allows participants to inspect an object without automatically committing to it. Spoken directions also carry the voice-identity cues needed for the self-similar comparison, unlike a nonverbal tone or a visual arrow alone.

This division of roles motivates the apparatus; the study does not compare modalities experimentally. Both voice conditions use the same gaze and controller inputs, and the design includes no unguided control. Recognition and comprehension cannot be inferred from a valid gaze intersection alone; they require behavioral evidence and participant reports. Tracking validity and calibration also constrain when gaze can inform assistance, as discussed in the eye-tracking quality section below.

\section{Gaze-Contingent Auditory Guidance}
Auditory guidance takes several forms. A sound can encode gaze error through loudness, indicate a location through spatial rendering, or communicate an instruction through words. Only the last of these necessarily carries the verbal and vocal identity cues central to this thesis.

Losing et al. tested gaze-contingent sonification in a small desktop study with ten participants: the loudness of a flute tone varied with gaze-to-target distance, reaching silence at the target~\cite{losing2014guiding}. Benefits depended on the search task, with the clearest improvement in the difficult number-search condition. The study supports linking auditory feedback to current gaze but offers no evidence about any particular spoken phrase. The present adaptation replaces the continuously varying tone with short spatial instructions so that participants hear the assigned voice throughout search. The frequency and timing of these instructions were set for this study rather than derived from the sonification work, and they have not yet been validated.

Kwok et al.'s Two-Step Gaze Guidance evaluated 69 participants using a three-monitor display and eye tracking, rather than a VR headset~\cite{kwok2022twostep}. Initial spatial-audio orientation followed by finer guidance shortened search times relative to the other tested conditions; accuracy did not differ significantly across conditions. Their implementation also buffered transitions between guidance stages. The transferable idea is a staged change in the information a cue supplies: first orient toward a region, then refine within it. Follow My Voice adapts that structure to wall entry and within-wall directions.

Binetti et al. provide a complementary headset-based example using visual arrows and spatialized alarm sounds to locate out-of-view targets~\cite{binetti2021outofview}. Adding sound mitigated response-time costs when visual cues and targets occupied different depth planes, but did not remove the corresponding accuracy cost. Because the study had no sound-only condition, it offers conditional support for an auditory channel when visual guidance is constrained rather than evidence that speech is superior to visual cues. Its separate response-time and accuracy results also motivate reporting selection errors separately from completion time in the present voice comparison.

Zhang et al.'s 96-participant AR book-search study more directly concerns verbal instructions~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. Its audio hint specified an approximate shelf once at task onset; it was not repeated gaze-contingent speech. In the sub-study without gaze-playback feedback, hint conditions outperformed the control but did not significantly differ from one another. Advantages of combined visual and audio hints over either modality alone appeared in the second task of the feedback sub-study. These results support examining task completion and workload together and stating cue timing and feedback exposure explicitly.

Sabouni et al. evaluated nonverbal proximity alerts during a VR search for target paintings~\cite{sabouni2025guidance}. A brief beep signaled entry into a target's vicinity. Compared with no cue, the audio condition reduced task-list checking, but it did not significantly reduce completion time relative to the control. The study therefore supports examining attention allocation alongside completion time.

Coughlan et al.'s CamIO system guided a tracked stylus toward points of interest on physical objects using cardinal-direction instructions~\cite{coughlan2020spatial}. Its feasibility study involved four blind participants. This provides a precedent for communicating spatial corrections through speech.

\paragraph{Design synthesis for the voice comparison.}
The agent first helps locate the relevant wall, then narrows the search within it; the participant confirms the inferred target with the right trigger. The target's initially hidden identity gives the voice an ongoing instructional role, unlike a single announcement supplementing a known target.

The design records guidance during search, acknowledgments of qualified gaze or controller contact, and feedback after selection as separate events. A success chime after selection cannot explain an earlier change of direction within the same trial, although it may shape later experience. The researcher-facing Trial Review tool also differs from Zhang et al.'s participant-facing gaze-playback intervention~\cite{zhang2023seehear}: researchers use it to audit delivery, and the protocol includes no participant playback.

\section{Context and Short-Term Memory in XR Agents}
A related AR navigation system by Yang et al. combines Building Information Modeling with retrieval-augmented generation and compares directional arrows alone with an embodied guide accompanied by the same arrows~\cite{yang2025embodiednavigation}. Its comparison concerns the presentation of navigation assistance, not self-similar voice. The walking task, destination queries, and embodied interface in that system differ from the seated hidden-target task used here.

EmBARDiment uses gaze-informed contextual memory to support interaction with an embodied XR agent~\cite{bovo2025embardiment}. That approach motivates making recent interaction evidence available to the assistance policy. Follow My Voice implements a narrower, bounded history of recent spatial progress, actual speech, and guidance transitions, with retention limits set for this application rather than taken from the cited system. The history holds no long-term autobiographical memory or inferred model of the participant's mental state. Unlike the cited interface, the guiding voice also has no visible agent body.

Work on memory-augmented AR agents and the GenLARP system illustrates further directions for context-aware XR agents~\cite{choi2025memory,yu2025genlarp}. The former is a conceptual framework and the latter a short system description, so neither supplies a validated timing threshold for this application. Research on user-aware virtual agents and proactive AR assistance likewise informs the design of state-dependent behavior~\cite{gunawardhana2024useraware,li2025satori}.

Short-term history is useful when the same current geometry can have different meanings. A target to the left may be one the participant has not yet approached, or one they have just passed while turning right. A recently delivered cue can make another identical cue unnecessary. History distinguishes these cases only if it records reliable observations and actual playback. Because this mechanism is shared across voices, the study cannot estimate its independent effect.

Feedback specificity offers a further distinction between adaptive assistance and generic encouragement. Zimmerer et al. examined performance-specific feedback from a virtual exercise coach and found changes in selected agent-perception measures without a corresponding effect on motivation~\cite{zimmerer2025feedback}. For Follow My Voice, this distinction motivates describing whether a cue reports observed progress, corrects direction, acknowledges qualified gaze or pointing, or confirms a successful selection. These functions are common to both voice conditions, so the study compares voice configurations rather than estimating an independent effect of feedback specificity.

\section{Eye-Tracking Quality and Interpretation}
Eye-tracking quality involves more than a nominal sampling frequency. The reporting guidance of Dunn et al. and the data-quality discussion of Niehorster et al. motivate reporting acquisition, validity, spatial performance, and missingness~\cite{dunn2024reporting,niehorster2026dataquality}. For this system, the relevant questions are how many distinct source observations arrive, how old they are when used, and how error varies across the search space.

Validation work on the Vive Pro Eye and tools such as GazeMetrics are methodological resources~\cite{schuetz2022vive,sipatchin2021usability,adhanom2020gazemetrics}. Their results do not transfer directly to the HTC Vive Focus Vision because device model, fit, calibration, coordinate conversion, and task geometry can differ. The validation protocol therefore uses independent targets across heights, boundaries, distances, and head orientations, kept separate from the targets used to estimate an offset correction.

Drewes et al. compare gaze during locomotion in virtual reality and the real world~\cite{drewes2021locomotion}. Because the present study is seated and relies on head and chair rotation rather than free walking, locomotion findings do not directly estimate gaze behavior in this task. Head motion is logged because it participates in search, and claims about eye events are limited to what the available measurements support.

Salvucci and Goldberg discuss methods for identifying fixations and saccades in eye-tracking data~\cite{salvucci2000fixations}. The 100-ms vicinity qualification used here governs interface acknowledgment and is not a validated fixation classifier; gaze contacts and qualified vicinity events are therefore reported as interaction measures.

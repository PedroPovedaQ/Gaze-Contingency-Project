
\section{Planned Statistical Analysis}
\label{ch:analysis}
The planned analysis follows the three research questions. For RQ1, active completion time is the primary outcome, with missed targets and NASA-TLX workload as secondary outcomes. For RQ2, pupillary activity, fixation count, and saccade frequency are analyzed once the eye-event detectors are validated. For RQ3, the IMI interest/enjoyment subscale measures engagement, with the other IMI subscales reported alongside it. Agent experience, reliance, and debrief responses are exploratory. Because each participant experiences both voices, these outcomes are compared within participants. Guidance diagnostics describe exposure and technical context. The decision model and gaze contingency are shared by both conditions, so the statistical comparison concerns voice configuration rather than the independent value of those components.

\subsection{Primary Contrast}
The primary estimand compares active completion time under the self-similar and neutral voices, standardized to the same starting-difficulty distribution. A trial-level mixed-effects analysis of log completion time accounts for participant clustering, voice condition, period/order, trial progression and absolute pre-cue starting theta. The analysis reports the voice time ratio and uncertainty using a two-sided contrast, even though H1 predicts faster search with the self-similar voice, so that an advantage for either voice can be detected.

A prespecified low-complexity function of theta represents nonlinearity in starting difficulty. Nominal-angle strata support balance plots and a categorical sensitivity analysis. Zero-degree trials remain in the analysis, and completion time is not divided by angle. Before confirmatory recruitment, simulation will finalize the model's random-effects structure, angle function and smallest meaningful effect. The participant count will be justified under that same model rather than by treating repeated trials as independent participants.

\subsection{A Candidate Primary Model}
The following model illustrates the intended estimand; the final specification will be fixed in the preregistration. For participant $i$, period $b$, and trial $j$, let $Y_{ibj}=\log T_{ibj}$ for successfully completed active-search intervals. A candidate specification is
\begin{align}
Y_{ibj}={}&\beta_0+\beta_V V_{ib}+\beta_B B_b+\beta_O O_i\\
&+f(|\theta_{0,ibj}|)+g(j)+u_{0i}+u_{Vi}V_{ib}+\epsilon_{ibj}.
\end{align}
Here $V$ indicates self-similar rather than neutral voice, $B$ identifies the second period, and $O$ identifies assigned voice order. The functions $f$ and $g$ represent prespecified low-complexity effects of exact starting angle and trial progression. The index $j$ denotes position within a block (1--40), and the period term captures the between-block change. The exact pre-cue angle comes from the snapshot described in Section~\ref{ch:measurement}; earlier development recordings provide only nominal wall angles. Participant intercepts and, if supported by the design and model stability, participant voice slopes represent clustering and variation in within-participant contrasts. With only one block per voice, the slope variance can also absorb block-specific session effects, so it does not isolate stable individual differences in voice responsiveness. The final random-effects structure will be selected on the basis of the experimental design and simulation rather than statistical significance~\cite{barr2013random}.

Under this model, the exponentiated voice coefficient, $\exp(\beta_V)$, is a conditional multiplicative time contrast, which need not equal a marginal arithmetic mean ratio. The primary estimand is therefore the marginal contrast standardized over the prespecified common starting-difficulty distribution, and the conditional coefficient is reported separately as a model parameter. Predictions on the original seconds scale require an explicit retransformation procedure and uncertainty estimate.

Counterbalancing the order of treatments across participants is a long-standing way to balance residual, or carryover, effects in repeated-measures designs~\cite{williams1949residual}. A two-period design supports adjustment for period and sequence, but it cannot independently identify every possible asymmetric carryover mechanism. For example, using a voice during practice could affect familiarity in its first block, and the second block could benefit from task learning. Period and order terms therefore address these influences only partially, and the report describes residual carryover as a limitation.

\subsection{Sample Size and Precision Planning}
Forty trials per block increase the information available from each participant. Because a participant's 80 trials are correlated observations, precision depends on the number of participants as well as the number of trials. The power analysis will reflect the planned trial-level model, between-participant heterogeneity, within-participant correlation, geometry distribution, incomplete trials, and the effect size worth detecting.

The planned simulation generates participants with both voice blocks and the intended nominal-angle quotas, varying plausible residual variation, participant intercept variation, voice-slope variation, and missingness. Each simulated dataset is fitted with the same model intended for the final analysis, and convergence, bias, interval coverage, and power or precision are summarized across candidate enrollment sizes. The smallest meaningful time ratio and its supporting assumptions will be documented before recruitment. Synthetic pilot values used to test the workflow are labeled as synthetic rather than presented as empirical variance estimates.

Pilot data can inform variance assumptions only if a prespecified rule determines whether those observations enter the confirmatory sample. Each session records a software-version and protocol identifier, so pilot data collected under a different guidance policy can be distinguished from data collected with the final study version.

\subsection{Secondary Outcomes and Multiplicity}
Condition-specific ratings are collected retrospectively after both blocks, with survey timestamps retained. Each rating set refers to one participant-by-block observation. Because ratings are not collected immediately after each block, recall and recency effects are considered when interpreting condition differences. Individual NASA-TLX workload dimensions and complete unweighted workload means~\cite{hart1988nasatlx} are summarized by participant and block. IMI subscales~\cite{mcauley1989imi} and the three six-item agent-perception constructs remain separate scores. The additional Guo Q20--22 appealing, eerie and familiar ratings~\cite{guo2024doppelganger} are analyzed individually rather than combined into an eeriness score. Voice similarity serves as a manipulation check, and custom experience ratings and self-reported guidance use are also analyzed individually.

The analysis retains paired observations and accounts for period where appropriate. The choice between ordinal-item and approximately continuous scale models will be justified for each measure before confirmatory analysis.

Multiplicity families, secondary contrasts, and adjustment rules will be specified before confirmatory analysis. Holm's procedure can be applied to a prespecified family of confirmatory secondary comparisons~\cite{holm1979simple} once the membership of that family has been fixed. Exploratory agent-perception and custom-item results are labeled as exploratory and reported with effect estimates and uncertainty rather than as a list of significance claims.

A participant who does not recognize the clone provides information about the strength of the manipulation and is not excluded solely on that basis. Similarly, low liking of either voice is an outcome rather than grounds for exclusion. Sensitivity analyses may examine synthesis quality or recognition. Conditioning on experience that follows assignment changes the interpretation of the contrast, however, and any such analysis states that change.

\subsection{Data Screening and Missingness}
Data screening first establishes structural integrity: unique participant/session/block/trial keys, consistent voice assignments, chronological reveal and selection events, and required geometry provenance. It then assesses observation coverage and protocol deviations.

Missing data affect each measure differently. A trial with sparse gaze coverage may have valid selection timing yet unreliable gaze-contact latency, so exclusions are measure-specific where justified. Completion time can remain valid when gaze contact is missing, and a complete motion stream cannot recover missing voice playback events. Each analysis reports its own denominator and reasons for missingness.

Stopping, timeout, withdrawal, and technical failure will be handled under prespecified rules. A complete-case time model estimates completion times among observed successful trials under its assumptions. It may not describe all assigned trials if incomplete outcomes differ by voice, so the final plan will also define a complementary completion-probability or time-to-event analysis for use if incompletion is consequential. The protocol sets the active-search deadline at 25 seconds. Deadline-expired attempts count as unsuccessful outcomes for completion probability and are right-censored at 25 active seconds in a time-to-event analysis, rather than treated as 25-second completion times. Earlier development recordings predate this rule and retain their original exposure.

Raw attempts, incorrect selections, pause intervals, timeouts, technical failures and withdrawals are all retained, and stopped trials are not coded as successful completion times. The deadline outcome is recorded separately from technical failure and withdrawal. The remaining treatment of those interruptions and the missing-data estimand will be specified before collection. Slow successful trials within the deadline are not removed solely for being slow. Missing responses are not replaced with zero, and questionnaire scores follow the complete-subscale rule, with item-level missingness reported.

\subsection{Behavior as Outcome Rather Than Automatic Adjustment}
Search behavior can be a consequence of the voice a participant hears. Instruction count, gaze path, and controller behavior may all change with voice condition, and including them automatically as primary-model covariates could remove part of the effect the study intends to estimate. The primary comparison therefore adjusts for pre-cue difficulty and design variables. Voice-by-theta variation, selected neutral voice, acoustic quality, guidance timing, and search behavior enter exploratory or sensitivity analyses with their assumptions reported.

For example, slower search can produce more opportunities for guidance, and more guidance can itself affect search time. A correlation between instruction count and duration does not establish that speech caused delay.

\subsection{Guidance Delivery Evaluation}
The delivery analysis distinguishes event qualification, decision latency, model deferral, scheduling delay and actual playback, and it evaluates gaze and controller confirmation separately. Sequence-level review includes what happened before and after each cue. Automated checks and ratings from an offline language-model evaluator (Appendix~\ref{app:scenarios}) serve as advisory system-level evidence and are distinct from participant perception scores.

\subsection{Additional Performance and Eye-Behavior Figures}
Missed-target percentages complement completion-time distributions, with the number of completed, timed-out, technically interrupted, and unresolved attempts shown explicitly. For the participant study, paired participant summaries by voice condition are preferable to treating all trial rows as independent observations. Development recordings collected without a deadline are reported separately from searches governed by the 25-second deadline.

After independent detector validation, possible additional figures include fixation-count distributions, saccade rates with their valid-time denominators, and IPA distributions~\cite{duchowski2018ipa} with retained pupil coverage, each separated by voice condition and participant. Last-target-fixation duration requires validated events and a target-area join. No EEG or ERP plot can be generated from the current headset logs. Until validation is complete, the candidate eye-measure figures in Section~\ref{sec:development-eye-candidates} serve only to inspect the processing pipeline and support no inference about voice effects or workload. Following eye-tracking reporting guidance~\cite{dunn2024reporting}, threshold sensitivity and missing-data summaries accompany each derived outcome.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis and Mixed Evidence}
The debrief begins with broad experience questions, followed by balanced probes about voice preference, perceived performance, comfort, and potential uses. The coding process records the initial codebook, revisions, coding decisions, and independent review of a subset. Contradictory or minority accounts are retained rather than forced into one favorable theme.

Reported reliance and observed behavior are considered together, and neither is treated as a perfect measure of the other. The final report separates quotations, coder interpretation, and behavioral evidence.

\subsection{Safety Outcomes and Manipulation Checks}
Safety reporting describes recorded discomfort reports, pauses, stops and incomplete exposure, including the reason for each interruption when available. These observations document tolerability and session completion rather than a standardized pre-to-post symptom score. The stopping procedure will be finalized before participant collection.

The similarity manipulation check reports the paired difference between self-similar and neutral-voice ratings with uncertainty. A group-level decision rule and any practically meaningful difference will be set in the preregistration. Because no equivalence test is specified, a nonsignificant voice contrast is not interpreted as equivalence.

\section{Motivation and Background}
Directing users' attention to objects of interest has long been a concern in virtual reality (VR), particularly when relevant content lies outside the user's current field of view. An immersive environment can surround a user with information, yet only part of that environment is visible at any moment. Grogorick et al. address this problem through subtle gaze guidance across an immersive scene, while HiveFive uses swarm motion to direct attention while preserving immersion~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,lange2020hivefive}. These approaches illustrate a recurring design problem: how can a system help users locate relevant content while allowing them to remain engaged with the surrounding environment?

\subsection{Guiding Users Toward Objects of Interest}
Object search gives attention guidance a practical purpose. Finding the relevant object is often a prerequisite for following an instruction or interacting with an environment. Binetti et al. motivate augmented-reality (AR) search assistance through maintenance, repair, instruction, and training, where a person must locate a target in the surrounding scene~\cite{binetti2021outofview}. Zhang et al. investigate the concrete example of finding a specified book among other books~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. Such tasks let researchers study how users connect a cue with its intended object without reproducing an entire workplace activity.

Chiossi et al.'s \emph{Searching Across Realities} uses object search to examine how the presentation of physical and virtual content affects search efficiency and attention allocation~\cite{chiossi2024searching}. This provides a methodological rationale for treating object search as a controlled task with behavioral and gaze measures. Their comparison concerns augmented reality and augmented virtuality, rather than voice identity; it motivates specifying the visual environment carefully while the present study varies the guiding voice.

When an object begins outside the field of view, assistance must first help the user orient toward a relevant region. Once that region becomes visible, the user still needs to distinguish the intended object from nearby distractors. This distinction between orientation and local search motivates considering guidance as an unfolding interaction rather than a single cue. Visual techniques offer ways to direct attention~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,lange2020hivefive}; auditory guidance provides another way to communicate where to look as that interaction develops.

\subsection{From Auditory Cues to Spoken Assistance}
Research on auditory guidance supports using sound to help users locate objects, although its benefits depend on the task and cue design. In head-mounted AR, Binetti et al. combined visual arrows with spatialized alarm sounds to guide users toward out-of-view targets~\cite{binetti2021outofview}. Adding sound mitigated response-time costs when visual cues and targets occupied different depth planes, but did not remove the corresponding accuracy cost. The study supports considering an auditory channel alongside visual guidance; it does not establish that speech alone is superior to visual cues.

Spoken hints provide a more direct precedent for the present task. Zhang et al.'s AR book-search study used an audio instruction identifying an approximate shelf~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. Hint conditions improved performance relative to the control in the sub-study without post-task feedback, while advantages of combined visual and audio hints depended on task and feedback exposure. Their verbal cue was delivered at task onset rather than continuously in response to gaze. The study therefore motivates spoken search assistance without establishing the effectiveness of the more adaptive interaction proposed here.

Gaze-contingent sonification and staged auditory guidance offer complementary precedents for adapting assistance to search progress. Losing et al. varied a tone with gaze-to-target distance, and Kwok et al. combined initial spatial-audio orientation with finer guidance~\cite{losing2014guiding,kwok2022twostep}. These studies used desktop displays rather than immersive headsets, and their cues should not be equated with conversational speech. Their relevance is the connection between what the user is currently looking at and what information the system supplies next.

Follow My Voice applies this idea through short spoken directions: first orient toward a target region, then refine the search within it. The agent knows the target, while the participant initially does not know which object to select. Speech consequently has an ongoing instructional role, progressively connecting a direction with an intended object. This provides a setting in which the user repeatedly encounters the guiding voice, raising a further question: does hearing that guidance in a voice resembling one's own change the experience or effectiveness of receiving help?

\subsection{Self-Similar Voice and the Doppelg\"anger Connection}
A spatial instruction conveys both a direction and a voice identity. The words ``look to your right'' specify an action, while the voice delivering them may be familiar, appealing, or uncomfortable. Self-similar synthesis uses recorded speech to produce a voice resembling the participant. Its relevance to guidance therefore extends beyond whether the instruction is intelligible: the resemblance may change how the participant experiences the agent giving that instruction.

Guo et al.'s \emph{Collaborating with my Doppelg\"anger} provides a direct motivation for investigating this possibility~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}. Their 25-participant VR study crossed self-similar appearance and voice during a jigsaw puzzle co-solving task with a virtual character. Self-similar voices increased likability and believability ratings while also increasing eeriness; believability additionally depended on the interaction between appearance and voice. These findings show why favorable impressions and discomfort should be examined separately. They do not establish that self-similar speech improves object-search speed or that the same responses will occur when the guide has no visible body.

The present study carries that question from an embodied collaborator to a voice serving as a spatial guide. In the puzzle task, participants interacted with a visible character; here, they will receive directions toward an initially unnamed object from an agent without a visible body. This change in role matters because the participant must use the speech to determine where to look and what to select. Whether self-resemblance makes those instructions easier to follow, more welcome, or more uncomfortable remains an empirical question. Research on self-similar avatar voices in an educational game supplies additional motivation through selected engagement and identification outcomes, while also underscoring that findings depend on the task and measures~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar}.

For designers, the comparison of interest is whether self-similar synthesis offers value beyond a voice the user already likes. Follow My Voice therefore compares a self-similar synthetic voice with a neutral voice selected by the participant from a supplied catalog. Throughout this thesis, \emph{neutral voice} names the comparison condition: an existing voice that is not cloned from the participant. The label does not imply an emotionally flat delivery, a lack of preference, or verified absence of perceived resemblance. Voice selection accommodates preference; cloning introduces resemblance to the participant. Both are practical ways to personalize an assistant, so the study asks what self-resemblance contributes when the alternative already reflects the user's choice. Because the voices can differ in acoustic properties as well as resemblance, the comparison concerns these two voice configurations rather than an isolated causal effect of similarity.

Usefulness will be evaluated through both behavior and experience. Recognizing the clone is a manipulation check, not a performance benefit; liking it without searching faster is still an experience result. Likewise, completing efficiently while finding the voice eerie would distinguish task effectiveness from acceptability. The thesis therefore examines completion time, workload, motivation, and agent experience separately. This approach follows the Doppelg\"anger findings in allowing favorable and adverse reactions to coexist, without assuming that self-similar guidance will be preferable.

\subsection{A Controlled Setting for Self-Similar Assistance}
Follow My Voice studies this comparison in a surrounding object-search task. A seated participant wears a virtual-reality headset and views colored geometric objects distributed across eight vertical planes. Some targets will initially lie outside the participant's field of view. The application knows which object is the target, while the participant is not given its color or shape at the start. Spoken directions first guide orientation toward the relevant wall and then refine the search within it. The participant aims a right-controller ray and presses the trigger to select the inferred target; gaze itself does not select an object.

The controlled shapes and surrounding layout make starting geometry and trial events easier to specify across the two voice conditions. Object search gives the speech a concrete purpose and observable outcomes, while the initially unnamed target makes guidance consequential throughout the task. Maintenance, training, and everyday retrieval motivate the broader value of search assistance, but transfer to those settings would require further evaluation.

The central comparison varies the assigned voice while the assistance mechanism stays common to both conditions. Gaze contingency and constrained agent decisions deliver relevant instructions but are not additional participant treatments. They provide the shared apparatus for asking whether hearing guidance in one's own synthetic voice changes the efficiency or experience of being guided.

\section{Problem Statement}
Together, the guidance literature and the self-similar voice literature motivate a question that neither establishes on its own: how will self-similar spoken assistance compare with a neutral voice when guiding search for an initially unnamed object? Auditory search studies motivate the use of sound and verbal hints~\cite{binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear}, while the Doppelg\"anger study motivates examining both favorable impressions and eeriness~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}. The unresolved issue is how those voice experiences relate to task performance and receiving repeated spatial instructions in the present setting.

The study therefore compares two practical voice configurations within a common task. It asks whether self-similar guidance changes active search time, workload, motivation, and the experience of the agent relative to neutral-voice guidance, keeping these outcomes separate rather than defining usefulness through a single favorable score. Because the voices can also differ in naturalness and other acoustic properties, it does not isolate resemblance as their only difference.

A credible answer requires appropriate guidance in both conditions and a record of what each participant actually heard. Gaze contingency, the shared decision policy, and playback logging serve that requirement. Independent gaze-quality checks and distinct records of looking, pointing, and selecting protect the interpretation of the interaction~\cite{dunn2024reporting,niehorster2026dataquality}. Chapters~\ref{ch:system} and~\ref{ch:measurement} describe these controls; the central empirical question remains the value of self-similar voice as assistance.

\section{Research Objectives and Questions}
The primary objective is to evaluate self-similar voice as a form of task assistance, using a neutral voice as the comparison within a shared gaze-contingent system. The primary research question is: will active search-completion time differ between self-similar and neutral voices after accounting for absolute starting angle and repeated observations within participants? The contrast is two-sided because the current design does not justify assuming a performance advantage for self-similarity.

Secondary questions concern perceived workload and activity experience. Will the two voices differ on the six workload dimensions and their complete unweighted mean? Will they differ on interest/enjoyment, effort/importance, pressure/tension, or value/usefulness? Because these outcomes concern different constructs, the analysis will preserve those distinctions rather than pool them into a broad satisfaction score.

Exploratory questions concern agent perception, reliance, and the relationship between task difficulty and voice experience. Participants may describe a voice as more responsive or humanlike, may rely on guidance more often, or may experience a cloned voice as familiar or eerie. Such reports will be treated as judgments about an interaction, not as evidence that the system possesses intelligence, emotions, awareness, or an inner voice.

A supporting validation requirement is to make the assistance process inspectable so that technical faults do not masquerade as effects of self-similar voice. Can recorded state and speech history explain why a cue was eligible, why it played or was suppressed, and how long it waited? Can evaluation distinguish a genuinely unhelpful instruction from an acceptable alternative wording? These questions concern system validity and readiness; they support the empirical study but do not replace it.

\section{Scope of the Comparison}
The independent variable is voice condition: neutral voice versus self-similar synthetic voice. Both conditions use gaze contingency, the same semantic phrase library, the same local geometry rules, and the same controller-selection mechanics. Consequently, the study will not isolate the benefit of gaze contingency, short-term memory, the cloud decision agent, or synthetic speech in general. Those are shared system properties.

The task is deliberately narrower than unrestricted navigation. Participants remain seated and rotate their head and chair to search surrounding planes. Guidance such as ``turn left'' concerns orientation within this setup; it does not ask them to walk toward a target. Objects will appear in a virtual room, and the target will initially be unnamed. Conclusions will therefore be bounded to guided search under these conditions.

The use of one practice trial in the first assigned voice is a current protocol decision. It introduces an exposure asymmetry: whichever voice appears first also appears during practice. Counterbalancing distributes that asymmetry across voices, but does not remove learning or carryover. The analysis must retain period and assigned order, and the report must describe the practice assignment explicitly.

\section{Contributions of This First Pass}
The thesis develops an empirical comparison of self-similar voice with a preferred alternative in virtual-reality task assistance. Its conceptual contribution is to distinguish resemblance from preference and to examine performance alongside the favorable or uncomfortable experience of hearing one's own synthetic voice. The study will not assume that greater perceived similarity implies faster search, lower workload, or a more welcome agent.

The methodological contribution is a within-participant design that makes this comparison operational: two counterbalanced voice blocks, a shared hidden-target task, explicit trigger selection, identical nominal starting-angle quotas across blocks, retrospective voice-referenced questionnaires, and a planned repeated-measures analysis. Manipulation checks and acoustic-quality reporting will help establish what the voice conditions actually delivered before their outcomes are interpreted.

The implemented guidance and recording platform supports that empirical contribution. Local spatial eligibility, constrained agent decisions, prepared speech, and synchronized review make it possible to inspect what each participant heard and whether delivery problems affected the comparison. The platform is the apparatus for studying self-similar voice; a provider benchmark or a new gaze-guidance algorithm is not the thesis's primary claim.

The empirical contribution remains pending. This draft does not report a recruited sample, condition means, significance tests, or participant quotations. A completed thesis will replace the pending results sections with the frozen study's actual voice-condition outcomes and revise its abstract and conclusion accordingly.

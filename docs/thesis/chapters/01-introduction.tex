\chapter{INTRODUCTION}
\label{ch:introduction}
\section{Motivation and Background}
Virtual agents are increasingly being designed to help people carry out tasks in virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality~\cite{bovo2025embardiment,madritsch2026assistance,venkatakrishnan2026clarifying}. When such an agent speaks, its voice becomes a design choice in its own right, and instant voice-cloning methods have made it practical for that voice to resemble a specific person, including the user~\cite{qin2023openvoice}. Self-similar voices, along with human voices measured as acoustically close to the listener's own, have some initial data showing that they can improve task behavior and user-perceived agent favorability~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar,jaggy2025similarity,guo2024doppelganger}; whether they also reduce workload has not been established and is a question this thesis attempts to answer.

We ask the question: When an agent helping you speaks in a voice like your own, does it impact task completion in virtual and augmented reality? A self-similar voice could make an agent's instructions more engaging to follow. It could also feel eerie~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}, which could have costs in enjoyability or attention, or it could have little impact at all. Each of these outcomes would be informative for anyone deciding how an agent should sound. We compare a Self-Similar Voice (SSV) with a Selected Agent Voice (SAV) that the participant chooses from a catalog, and we evaluate the impact on task performance, workload, and engagement.

\subsection{Object Search}
To answer this question we need a task in which a person works with an agent repeatedly and in which performance can be measured. An immersive environment can surround the user with relevant content, such as targets, instructions, or points of interest, yet this content must often be brought to the user's attention before it can be inspected or acted on. Object search was chosen as our method of investigation because visual search is an everyday task and has been studied extensively in VR and augmented reality head-mounted displays~\cite{gruenefeld2017eyesee360,trepkowski2019fov,chiossi2024searching,quinn2024review,kelley2025cueing} and requires gathering and using gaze-aware agentic guidance, as the user does not know initially what the object of interest is. Researchers have investigated a variety of methods of agentic guidance, including subtle gaze guidance that steers attention across an immersive scene~\cite{grogorick2017subtle}, motion cues that direct attention while preserving immersion~\cite{lange2020hivefive}, audio alerts that draw attention to objects of interest~\cite{sabouni2025guidance}, and spoken directions~\cite{tanaka2026coarse}. This paper investigates spoken guidance specifically because it allows us to investigate effects between agentic voice presentation.

In Follow My Voice, participants search for objects while seated in VR. Colored geometric objects are distributed across eight vertical planes around the participant, following prior visual search studies in mixed reality and in 360-degree VR~\cite{chiossi2024searching,kelley2025cueing}. Some targets initially lie outside the participant's field of view. The agent knows which object is the target, while the participant is not given its color or shape at the start. Spoken directions first guide the user toward the general vicinity of the object and then instructions become more fine-grained as the user's gaze approaches the general vicinity of the target. The participant aims a right-controller ray and presses the trigger to select the inferred target to complete a trial.

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:research-questions}
The primary objective of this thesis is to evaluate whether working with an agent that speaks in an SSV, rather than an SAV chosen by the participant, changes how people perform a task and how they experience it. To measure this, we use a gaze-contingent agent and an object search task that requires agentic guidance. This thesis addresses three research questions, each with a corresponding hypothesis:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.9em,labelwidth=3.5em,labelsep=0.4em,align=left]
\item[\textbf{RQ1:}] Does voice self-similarity impact performance and perceived workload (NASA-TLX)?
\item[\textbf{H1:}] Guidance in the SSV leads to faster search and lower perceived workload than guidance in the SAV.
\item[\textbf{RQ2:}] Does voice self-similarity impact eye-tracking indicators of workload (IPA) and of visual search efficiency (fixations and saccades)?
\item[\textbf{H2:}] The SSV yields more efficient visual search, with fewer fixations and saccades and lower pupillary activity.
\item[\textbf{RQ3:}] Does voice self-similarity impact engagement in the object search task?
\item[\textbf{H3:}] The SSV makes the search task more engaging.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Statement}
A growing body of work examines how self-similar voices influence the experience of interacting with avatars and virtual characters~\cite{guo2024doppelganger,kao2021selfsimilar}. Less is known about whether such a voice changes how well people carry out a task with an agent's help. Previous research on object search, in which visual and auditory guidance direct attention toward objects of interest, including targets outside the user's current field of view~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,lange2020hivefive,gruenefeld2017eyesee360,marquardt2020guidance,binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear}, provides an established task in which to ask that question.

This study explores the impact of a self-similar agent voice, using search for an initially unnamed object in VR as the task. It evaluates how receiving spatial instructions in the SSV, compared with the SAV, affects task performance and the experience of doing the task. The comparison considers search performance and perceived workload, eye-tracking indicators of workload and search efficiency, and engagement.

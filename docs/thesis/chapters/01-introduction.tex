\chapter{INTRODUCTION}
\label{ch:introduction}
\section{Motivation and Background}
Directing a user's attention to objects of interest is a persistent challenge in virtual reality (VR). An immersive environment can surround the user with relevant content, such as targets, instructions, or points of interest, yet only the portion within the current field of view is visible at any moment. Content outside that view must first be brought to the user's attention before it can be inspected or acted on. Prior work has addressed this through subtle gaze guidance that steers attention across an immersive scene~\cite{grogorick2017subtle}, through motion cues that direct attention while preserving immersion~\cite{lange2020hivefive}, and through audio alerts that draw attention to objects of interest~\cite{sabouni2025guidance}. These approaches share a common design problem: how can a system help users locate relevant content without pulling them out of the surrounding environment?

\subsection{Guiding Users Toward Objects of Interest}
The present study explores how the voice used to deliver instructional guidance influences task performance, perceived workload, and enjoyment. It compares self-similar and neutral voices while keeping the content and mechanism of guidance consistent across conditions.

Binetti et al. examine augmented-reality (AR) search assistance motivated by maintenance, repair, instruction, and training, settings in which a person must locate a target in the surrounding scene~\cite{binetti2021outofview}. Zhang et al. examine the concrete example of finding a specified book among other books~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. Such tasks let researchers study how users connect a cue with its intended object without reproducing an entire workplace activity.

Chiossi et al.'s \emph{Searching Across Realities} also studies object search, providing a methodological rationale for using it as a controlled task with behavioral and gaze measures~\cite{chiossi2024searching}. Their study examines how the presentation of physical and virtual content affects search efficiency and attention allocation. For the present comparison, this work motivates specifying the visual environment carefully so that the guiding voice can be varied within a consistent search scene.

When an object begins outside the field of view, assistance must first support attention orientation, directing the user toward a relevant region of the scene~\cite{sabouni2025guidance}. Once that region becomes visible, the user still needs to distinguish the intended object from nearby distractors.

\subsection{From Auditory Cues to Spoken Assistance}
Speech has been studied as a way to interact with three-dimensional space since Bolt's \emph{Put-That-There}, which combined spoken commands with pointing to place and move objects in a spatial display~\cite{bolt1980putthatthere}. Spoken guidance in the other direction, from system to user, was soon compared with maps for directing drivers through unfamiliar areas~\cite{streeter1985tell}. Advances in virtual and augmented reality have renewed this line of work. Augmented reality systems combine spoken and spatial cues to direct attention toward objects~\cite{zhang2023seehear,binetti2021outofview} and ground spoken instructions in the user's live view~\cite{kim2026speechtospatial}, and virtual reality studies use spoken directions to guide users toward targets~\cite{tanaka2026coarse}. The same technology could improve quality of life for blind and low-vision people, for whom spoken spatial guidance can make locating objects more independent~\cite{coughlan2020spatial,tanaka2026coarse}.

Research on auditory guidance supports using sound to help users locate objects. In head-mounted AR, Binetti et al. combined visual arrows with spatialized alarm sounds to guide users toward out-of-view targets~\cite{binetti2021outofview}.

A VR exhibition study compared a rearview mirror, a proximity beep, and a controller vibration against a no-cue control while participants searched for seven target paintings~\cite{sabouni2025guidance}. Audio and haptic alerts reduced how often participants consulted their target list, yet neither shortened completion time relative to the control. These alerts carried minimal information, signaling only that a target was nearby. Spoken guidance can instead specify where to look, which motivates examining speech as a richer channel for orienting attention.

Previous studies have examined using spoken hints to help identify an object. Zhang et al.'s AR book-search study used an audio instruction identifying an approximate shelf~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. In VR, Tanaka et al. supplemented continuous spatial audio with on-demand spoken directions such as ``up'' while blind participants reached for targets; this verbal mode produced the lowest error rates, fastest completion, lowest workload, and strongest preference, whereas spatial audio mainly supported the initial coarse search~\cite{tanaka2026coarse}.

Other studies have used the user's gaze data to generate tonal audio that guides them toward a target object. Losing et al. varied a tone with gaze-to-target distance, and Kwok et al. combined initial spatial-audio orientation with finer guidance~\cite{losing2014guiding,kwok2022twostep}. Both studies used desktop displays rather than immersive headsets, and their nonverbal cues differ from conversational speech. Their relevance here is the principle of linking the user's current gaze to the information the system supplies next.

Follow My Voice applies this principle through short spoken directions that first orient the participant toward a target region and then refine the search within it. Because the agent knows the target while the participant initially does not know which object to select, the participant depends on the agent's gaze-based instructions to find it. The participant therefore hears the guiding voice repeatedly during search, which raises a further question: does hearing that guidance in a voice resembling one's own change the experience or effectiveness of receiving help?

\subsection{Self-Similar Voice and the Doppelgänger Connection}
The voice delivering an instruction can influence how users experience an interaction as well as how they engage with a task. Listeners recognize personality in synthesized speech and respond more favorably to synthetic voices whose personality resembles their own~\cite{nass2001personality}. Work on virtual health assistants has examined how vocal delivery shapes user engagement~\cite{maxim2025modulation}. The user's own voice has also emerged as a persuasive medium: AI-generated encouragement in one's own voice was perceived as uniquely engaging and personal~\cite{fang2025emotional}. Studies of self-similar voices have reported differences in game performance, immersion, and perceptions of a virtual collaborator~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar,guo2024doppelganger}. In Follow My Voice, self-similar synthesis creates a cloned voice resembling the participant, allowing the study to examine whether that resemblance changes the experience or effectiveness of receiving spatial instructions.

Kao et al. provide evidence that voice resemblance can affect both behavior and experience~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar}. In an online programming game with 657 analyzed participants, players assigned a self-similar avatar voice completed more puzzles and played for longer than those assigned a dissimilar voice. They also reported greater similarity identification, competence, relatedness, and immersion, although direct differences in the measured intrinsic-motivation subscales were not significant. Performance in that study meant the number of puzzles completed during voluntary play, rather than faster completion of a fixed task.

Guo et al.'s \emph{Collaborating with my Doppelgänger} brings this question into immersive VR~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}. Their 25-participant study crossed self-similar appearance and voice while participants solved a jigsaw puzzle with a virtual character. Self-similar voices increased the character's likability and believability, but also elicited greater eeriness. This combination of favorable impressions and discomfort is particularly relevant to a guide that speaks repeatedly throughout a task.

Evidence from voice listening also connects resemblance with social judgments. Jaggy et al. found that recordings of other speakers whose voices were more similar to a listener's own tended to receive higher likability and trustworthiness ratings~\cite{jaggy2025similarity}. Because this experiment used recorded human voices rather than cloned agents delivering instructions, it supports a possible link between vocal resemblance and agent impressions, rather than demonstrating improved task assistance.

Voice choice offers a related, but distinct, form of personalization. In \emph{Audio Matters Too}, Kao et al. found that choosing an avatar's voice improved identification and perceived autonomy when participants could also choose its visual appearance; those benefits were not observed without visual choice~\cite{kao2022audiomatters}. Follow My Voice compares a self-similar cloned voice with a neutral voice selected by the participant from the available options. Here, \emph{neutral voice} denotes an existing voice not cloned from the participant; it does not imply emotionally flat speech or an absence of preference. The comparison asks whether self-similar synthesis offers value beyond a voice the user has already chosen, while recognizing that the two voices can differ in naturalness and other acoustic properties as well as resemblance.

The present study extends this literature from an avatar or embodied collaborator to a voice that guides search for an initially unnamed object. Participants use its spatial instructions to determine where to look and what to select, while the agent has no visible body. This thesis asks whether the object-search experience changes when that guidance is spoken in a self-similar voice rather than in a neutral voice the participant has chosen, in terms of search performance, measured as active completion time; enjoyment, measured with the interest/enjoyment subscale of the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory; and workload, measured with NASA-TLX. Perceived voice-similarity ratings check the intended resemblance, and agent experience is examined as an exploratory outcome.

\subsection{A Controlled Setting for Self-Similar Assistance}
Follow My Voice examines this comparison through seated object search in virtual reality. Colored geometric objects are distributed across eight vertical planes around the participant. Some targets initially lie outside the participant's field of view. The application knows which object is the target, while the participant is not given its color or shape at the start. Spoken directions first guide orientation toward the relevant wall and then refine the search within it. The participant aims a right-controller ray and presses the trigger to select the inferred target; gaze itself does not select an object.

Simple geometric shapes on a common arrangement of surrounding planes make starting geometry and trial events easier to specify consistently across the two voice conditions. Object search gives the speech a concrete purpose with observable outcomes, and because the target is initially unnamed, guidance remains consequential until the participant selects it.

The central comparison varies the assigned voice while keeping the assistance mechanism common to both conditions. Gaze contingency and constrained agent decisions use the current search state to select instructions.

Spoken guidance also offers a relevant direction for low-vision assistance because spatial instructions can convey information that would otherwise rely on visible markers. Coughlan et al.'s work with blind users demonstrates the feasibility of verbal corrections for locating points of interest on physical objects~\cite{coughlan2020spatial}. The present method offers a controlled way to study such instruction, but its visual targets and gaze-dependent feedback require separate accessibility testing. An extension for people with low vision would need to consider residual vision, target contrast and size, tracking reliability, and alternatives to gaze-dependent readiness and controller aiming.

\section{Problem Statement}
Previous research explores how visual and auditory guidance can direct attention toward objects of interest, including targets outside the user's current field of view~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,lange2020hivefive,binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear}. A related body of work examines how self-similar voices influence the experience of interacting with a virtual agent, including favorable impressions and eeriness~\cite{guo2024doppelganger,kao2021selfsimilar}. Together, these areas motivate examining self-similar spoken assistance in a task where instructions help users discover which object they are seeking.

This study explores the impact of self-similar spoken assistance when guiding search for an initially unnamed object in virtual reality. It evaluates how receiving spatial instructions in a self-similar synthetic voice, compared with a participant-selected neutral voice, affects task performance and the experience of performing the search. The comparison considers active search-completion time, perceived workload, motivation, and agent experience while keeping the task and guidance mechanism consistent across conditions. Because the voices can also differ in naturalness and other acoustic properties, resemblance is not the only difference between conditions.

Answering this question requires appropriate guidance in both conditions and a record of what each participant heard. Gaze contingency, the shared decision policy, and playback logging address this requirement. Interpreting the interaction also requires independent gaze-quality checks and separate records of looking, pointing, and selecting~\cite{dunn2024reporting,niehorster2026dataquality}. Chapter~\ref{ch:system}, including Section~\ref{ch:measurement}, describes these controls.

\section{Research Objectives and Questions}
The primary objective is to evaluate self-similar voice as a form of task assistance, using a neutral voice as the comparison within a shared gaze-contingent system. The primary research question is: does active search-completion time differ between self-similar and neutral voices after accounting for absolute starting angle and repeated observations within participants? The contrast is two-sided because no performance advantage for self-similarity is assumed.

Secondary questions concern perceived workload and activity experience. Will the two voices differ on the six workload dimensions and their complete unweighted mean? Will they differ on interest/enjoyment, effort/importance, pressure/tension, or value/usefulness? Because these outcomes concern different constructs, the analysis reports them separately rather than pooling them into a broad satisfaction score.

Exploratory questions concern agent perception, reliance, and the relationship between task difficulty and voice experience. Participants may describe a voice as more responsive or humanlike, may rely on guidance more often, or may experience a cloned voice as familiar or eerie.

A supporting objective is to make the assistance process inspectable so that technical faults can be separated from effects of self-similar voice. Can recorded state and speech history explain why a cue was eligible, why it played or was suppressed, and how long it waited? Can the evaluation distinguish an unhelpful instruction from an acceptable alternative wording?


\section{Scope of the Comparison}
The independent variable is voice condition: neutral voice versus self-similar synthetic voice. Both conditions use gaze contingency, the same semantic phrase library, the same local geometry rules, and the same controller-selection mechanics. Because these properties are shared, the study cannot estimate the separate contributions of gaze contingency, short-term memory, the cloud decision agent, or synthetic speech in general.

The task is deliberately narrower than unrestricted navigation. Participants remain seated and rotate their head and chair to search surrounding planes. Guidance such as ``turn left'' refers to orientation within this seated setup rather than walking toward a target. Objects appear in a virtual room, and the target is initially unnamed. Conclusions are limited to guided search under these conditions.

The single practice trial uses the first assigned voice, so whichever voice appears first receives additional exposure. Counterbalancing distributes this asymmetry across voices but does not remove learning or carryover; the analysis therefore models period and assigned order, and the practice assignment is reported.

\section{Intended Contributions}
The thesis develops an empirical comparison of self-similar voice with a preferred alternative in virtual-reality task assistance. Its conceptual contribution is to distinguish resemblance from preference and to examine performance alongside the favorable or uncomfortable experience of hearing one's own synthetic voice.

The methodological contribution is a within-participant design for this comparison: two counterbalanced voice blocks, a shared hidden-target task, explicit trigger selection, identical nominal starting-angle quotas across blocks, retrospective voice-referenced questionnaires, and a planned repeated-measures analysis. Manipulation checks and acoustic-quality reporting characterize what each voice condition delivered before outcomes are interpreted.

The guidance and recording platform serves as the apparatus for this comparison. Local spatial eligibility, constrained agent decisions, prepared speech, and synchronized review allow inspection of what each participant heard and whether delivery problems affected the comparison. Neither a provider benchmark nor a new gaze-guidance algorithm is claimed as a primary contribution.

The empirical contribution is pending; the thesis does not yet report a recruited sample, condition means, significance tests, or participant quotations.

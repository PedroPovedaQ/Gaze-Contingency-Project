\chapter{INTRODUCTION}
\label{ch:introduction}
\section{Motivation and Background}
Directing a user's attention to objects or areas of interest has long been a concern in virtual reality (VR). An immersive environment can surround the user with relevant content, such as targets, instructions, or points of interest, yet this content can remain inaccessible to the user for a variety of reasons. In some cases, objects are obscured; in others, the user may simply have been attending to a different part of the scene. As a result, relevant content must often be brought to the user's attention before it can be inspected or acted on. Researchers have addressed this through a variety of methods, including subtle gaze guidance that steers attention across an immersive scene~\cite{grogorick2017subtle}, motion cues that direct attention while preserving immersion~\cite{lange2020hivefive}, and audio alerts that draw attention to objects of interest~\cite{sabouni2025guidance}. In this thesis we focus on audio, as spoken guidance has been shown to be effective on its own~\cite{tanaka2026coarse}, and it allows us to investigate the relationship between a self-similar voice and the search experience. Self-similar voices, along with voices that listeners perceive as similar to their own, have some initial data showing that they can boost engagement and favorable impressions~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar,fang2025emotional,jaggy2025similarity,guo2024doppelganger}; whether they also reduce workload has not been established. This study evaluates the impact of different guiding voices on object search performance, workload, and engagement.

\subsection{Guiding Users Toward Objects of Interest}
Object search in virtual and augmented reality has been studied extensively in head-mounted displays~\cite{gruenefeld2017eyesee360,trepkowski2019fov,chiossi2024searching,quinn2024review,kelley2025cueing}. This has utility in augmented reality, teleoperation, and simulation training~\cite{binetti2021outofview}. Researchers have investigated a variety of methods to point individuals toward objects in virtual reality, from visual~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,gruenefeld2017eyesee360} to auditory~\cite{marquardt2020guidance,tanaka2026coarse}, or a combination of both~\cite{binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear}.

When an object begins outside the field of view, assistance must first support attention orientation, directing the user toward a relevant region of the scene~\cite{sabouni2025guidance}. Once that region becomes visible, the user still needs to distinguish the intended object from nearby distractors.

Speech has been studied as one potential way to interact with three-dimensional space in this way since Bolt's seminal work \emph{Put-That-There}, which combined spoken commands with pointing to place and move objects in a spatial display~\cite{bolt1980putthatthere}. Advances in virtual and augmented reality have renewed this area of research~\cite{zhang2023seehear,kim2026speechtospatial,tanaka2026coarse}, with possibilities for engagement with the environment being essentially endless. In addition, audio-based methods specifically could improve quality of life for blind and low-vision people, for whom spoken spatial guidance can make locating objects easier, helping make their lives more independent~\cite{coughlan2020spatial,tanaka2026coarse}.

Research on auditory guidance supports using sound to help users locate objects. In head-mounted AR, Binetti et al. combined visual arrows with spatialized alarm sounds to guide users toward out-of-view targets~\cite{binetti2021outofview}. On narrow field-of-view AR displays, Marquardt et al. found audio-tactile guidance slower than a comparable visualization technique but equally accurate, with users exhibiting better situational awareness~\cite{marquardt2020guidance}. In addition, Zhang et al.'s AR book-search study used audio and visual hints to help identify a specific book on a shelf~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. In VR, Tanaka et al. supplemented continuous spatial audio with on-demand spoken directions such as ``up'' while blind participants reached for targets; this verbal mode produced the lowest error rates, fastest completion, lowest workload, and strongest preference~\cite{tanaka2026coarse}. We take heavy inspiration from this type of vocal guidance in our research.

Follow My Voice applies this principle, starting with coarser spoken directions that orient the participant toward a particular target region, after which more precise hints refine the search within it~\cite{kwok2022twostep,tanaka2026coarse}. The agent, which is gaze-contingent, knows the whereabouts of the target, while the participant initially does not know which object to select. This creates a dependency on the agent's gaze-based instructions to find it.

\subsection{Self-Similar Voice}
Recent research indicates that the voice delivering an instruction can influence how users experience an interaction as well as how they engage with a task. Listeners recognize personality in synthesized speech and respond more favorably to synthetic voices whose personality resembles their own~\cite{nass2001personality}. The user's own self-similar voice has also emerged as a persuasive medium: AI-generated encouragement in one's own voice was perceived as uniquely engaging and personal, motivating the user~\cite{fang2025emotional}. Furthermore, another pioneering study showed that a self-similar voice made a virtual collaborator more likable and believable, though also more eerie~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}. Instant voice-cloning methods have made it practical to synthesize a voice resembling a specific speaker~\cite{qin2023openvoice}. In Follow My Voice, self-similar synthesis creates a cloned voice resembling the participant, allowing the study to examine whether that resemblance changes the experience or effectiveness of receiving spatial instructions in an object search scenario.

This thesis asks whether the object-search experience changes when that guidance is spoken in a self-similar voice rather than in a neutral voice the participant has chosen, in terms of performance and perceived workload, eye-tracking indicators of workload and search efficiency, and engagement in the search task (Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}).

\subsection{A Controlled Setting for Self-Similar Assistance}
Follow My Voice examines this comparison through seated object search in virtual reality. Colored geometric objects are distributed across eight vertical planes around the participant. Some targets initially lie outside the participant's field of view. The application knows which object is the target, while the participant is not given its color or shape at the start. Spoken directions first guide orientation toward the relevant wall and then refine the search within it. The participant aims a right-controller ray and presses the trigger to select the inferred target; gaze itself does not select an object.

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:research-questions}
The primary objective is to evaluate self-similar voice as a form of task assistance, using a neutral voice as the comparison within a shared gaze-contingent system. This thesis addresses three research questions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.2em]
\item[\textbf{RQ1:}] Does voice self-similarity impact performance and perceived workload (NASA-TLX)?
\item[\textbf{RQ2:}] Does voice self-similarity impact eye-tracking indicators of workload (IPA) and of visual search efficiency (fixations and saccades)?
\item[\textbf{RQ3:}] Does voice self-similarity impact engagement in the object search task?
\end{itemize}

The central hypothesis is that a self-similar voice makes guidance more engaging, and that this engagement improves search. Prior work supports this expectation: players assigned a self-similar avatar voice completed more puzzles and reported greater immersion~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar}, AI-generated encouragement in one's own voice was perceived as uniquely engaging~\cite{fang2025emotional}, and self-similar voices increased a virtual collaborator's likability and believability~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}. The hypotheses are therefore:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.2em]
\item[\textbf{H1:}] Guidance in the self-similar voice leads to faster search and lower perceived workload than guidance in the neutral voice.
\item[\textbf{H2:}] The self-similar voice yields more efficient visual search, with fewer fixations and saccades and lower pupillary activity.
\item[\textbf{H3:}] The self-similar voice makes the search task more engaging.
\end{itemize}

Although the hypotheses are directional, the voice contrasts are tested two-sided, so an advantage for the neutral voice would also be detected. The eye-tracking indicators for RQ2 are interpreted only after the detectors are validated on the headset (Section~\ref{ch:measurement}). Guo et al. also found that self-similar voices elicited greater eeriness~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}, so agent perception, including eeriness, is examined as an exploratory outcome alongside the voice-similarity manipulation check.

A supporting objective is to make the assistance process inspectable so that technical faults can be separated from effects of self-similar voice. Can recorded state and speech history explain why a cue was eligible, why it played or was suppressed, and how long it waited? Can the evaluation distinguish an unhelpful instruction from an acceptable alternative wording?


\section{Problem Statement}
Previous research explores how visual and auditory guidance can direct attention toward objects of interest, including targets outside the user's current field of view~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,lange2020hivefive,gruenefeld2017eyesee360,marquardt2020guidance,binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear}. A related body of work examines how self-similar voices influence the experience of interacting with a virtual agent, including favorable impressions and eeriness~\cite{guo2024doppelganger,kao2021selfsimilar}. Together, these areas motivate examining self-similar spoken assistance in a task where instructions help users discover which object they are seeking.

This study explores the impact of self-similar spoken assistance when guiding search for an initially unnamed object in virtual reality. It evaluates how receiving spatial instructions in a self-similar synthetic voice, compared with a participant-selected neutral voice, affects task performance and the experience of performing the search. The comparison considers search performance and perceived workload, eye-tracking indicators of workload and search efficiency, and engagement, while keeping the task and guidance mechanism consistent across conditions. Because the voices can also differ in naturalness and other acoustic properties, resemblance is not the only difference between conditions.

Answering this question requires appropriate guidance in both conditions and a record of what each participant heard. Gaze contingency, the shared decision policy, and playback logging address this requirement. Interpreting the interaction also requires independent gaze-quality checks and separate records of looking, pointing, and selecting~\cite{dunn2024reporting,niehorster2026dataquality}. Chapter~\ref{ch:system} and Section~\ref{ch:measurement} describe these controls.

\section{Scope of the Comparison}
The independent variable is voice condition: neutral voice versus self-similar synthetic voice. Both conditions use gaze contingency, the same semantic phrase library, the same local geometry rules, and the same controller-selection mechanics. Because these properties are shared, the study cannot estimate the separate contributions of gaze contingency, short-term memory, the cloud decision agent, or synthetic speech in general.

The task is deliberately narrower than unrestricted navigation. Participants remain seated and rotate their head and chair to search surrounding planes. Guidance such as ``turn left'' refers to orientation within this seated setup rather than walking toward a target. Objects appear in a virtual room, and the target is initially unnamed. Conclusions are limited to guided search under these conditions.

The single practice trial uses the first assigned voice, so whichever voice appears first receives additional exposure. Counterbalancing distributes this asymmetry across voices but does not remove learning or carryover; the analysis therefore models period and assigned order, and the practice assignment is reported.

\section{Intended Contributions}
The thesis develops an empirical comparison of self-similar voice with a preferred alternative in virtual-reality task assistance. Its conceptual contribution is to distinguish resemblance from preference and to examine performance alongside the favorable or uncomfortable experience of hearing one's own synthetic voice.

The methodological contribution is a within-participant design for this comparison: two counterbalanced voice blocks, a shared hidden-target task, explicit trigger selection, identical nominal starting-angle quotas across blocks, retrospective voice-referenced questionnaires, and a planned repeated-measures analysis. Manipulation checks and acoustic-quality reporting characterize what each voice condition delivered before outcomes are interpreted.

The guidance and recording platform serves as the apparatus for this comparison. Local spatial eligibility, constrained agent decisions, prepared speech, and synchronized review allow inspection of what each participant heard and whether delivery problems affected the comparison. Neither a provider benchmark nor a new gaze-guidance algorithm is claimed as a primary contribution.

The empirical contribution is pending; the thesis does not yet report a recruited sample, condition means, significance tests, or participant quotations.

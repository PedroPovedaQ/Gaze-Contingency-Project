\chapter{INTRODUCTION}
\label{ch:introduction}
\section{Motivation and Background}
Virtual agents are increasingly being designed to help people carry out tasks in virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality~\cite{bovo2025embardiment,madritsch2026assistance,venkatakrishnan2026clarifying}. When such an agent speaks, its voice becomes a design choice in its own right, and instant voice-cloning methods have made it practical for that voice to resemble a specific person, including the user~\cite{qin2023openvoice}. Self-similar voices, along with human voices measured as acoustically close to the listener's own, have some initial data showing that they can improve task behavior and favorable impressions~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar,jaggy2025similarity,guo2024doppelganger}; whether they also reduce workload has not been established.

This raises a broad question: does work go better, worse, or no differently when the agent helping you speaks in a voice like your own? A self-similar voice could make an agent's instructions more engaging to follow. It could also feel eerie~\cite{guo2024doppelganger}, which could cost comfort or attention, or it could have little impact at all. Each of these outcomes would be informative for anyone deciding how an agent should sound. We compare a Self-Similar Voice (SSV) with a Selected Agent Voice (SAV) that the participant chooses from a catalog, and we evaluate the impact on task performance, workload, and engagement.

\subsection{Object Search as the Experimental Task}
To answer this question we need a task in which a person works with an agent repeatedly and in which performance can be measured. We use object search. Visual search is an everyday task and has been studied extensively in VR and augmented reality head-mounted displays~\cite{gruenefeld2017eyesee360,trepkowski2019fov,chiossi2024searching,quinn2024review,kelley2025cueing}. An immersive environment can surround the user with relevant content, such as targets, instructions, or points of interest, yet this content must often be brought to the user's attention before it can be inspected or acted on. Researchers have addressed this through a variety of methods, including subtle gaze guidance that steers attention across an immersive scene~\cite{grogorick2017subtle}, motion cues that direct attention while preserving immersion~\cite{lange2020hivefive}, audio alerts that draw attention to objects of interest~\cite{sabouni2025guidance}, and spoken directions~\cite{tanaka2026coarse}. Spoken guidance is what makes the task useful here, because it places the agent's voice at the center of the work.

Object search is therefore the mechanism of this study rather than its subject. We do not set out to build a better search technique, and the search task and guidance are the same in both voice conditions. Completion time gives a direct measure of performance, the task can be repeated over many trials, and eye tracking records how each search unfolds.

In Follow My Voice, participants search for objects while seated in VR. Colored geometric objects are distributed across eight vertical planes around the participant. Some targets initially lie outside the participant's field of view. The application knows which object is the target, while the participant is not given its color or shape at the start, so the participant relies on the agent's voice on every trial. Spoken directions first guide orientation toward the relevant object plane and then refine the search within it. The participant aims a right-controller ray and presses the trigger to select the inferred target; gaze itself does not select an object.

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:research-questions}
The primary objective of this thesis is to evaluate whether working with an agent that speaks in an SSV, rather than an SAV chosen by the participant, changes how people perform a task and how they experience it. Object search in 3D serves as the task, with both voices sharing the same gaze-contingent system for evaluation. This thesis addresses three research questions, each with a corresponding hypothesis:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.9em,labelwidth=3.5em,labelsep=0.4em,align=left]
\item[\textbf{RQ1:}] Does voice self-similarity impact performance and perceived workload (NASA-TLX)?
\item[\textbf{H1:}] Guidance in the SSV leads to faster search and lower perceived workload than guidance in the SAV.
\item[\textbf{RQ2:}] Does voice self-similarity impact eye-tracking indicators of workload (IPA) and of visual search efficiency (fixations and saccades)?
\item[\textbf{H2:}] The SSV yields more efficient visual search, with fewer fixations and saccades and lower pupillary activity.
\item[\textbf{RQ3:}] Does voice self-similarity impact engagement in the object search task?
\item[\textbf{H3:}] The SSV makes the search task more engaging.
\end{itemize}

Although the hypotheses predict a benefit, the primary comparison is tested two-sided, so that an advantage for either voice can be detected (Section~\ref{sec:analysis-plan}).

\section{Problem Statement}
A growing body of work examines how self-similar voices influence the experience of interacting with avatars and virtual characters, including favorable impressions and eeriness~\cite{guo2024doppelganger,kao2021selfsimilar}. Less is known about whether such a voice changes how well people carry out a task with an agent's help. Previous research on object search, in which visual and auditory guidance direct attention toward objects of interest, including targets outside the user's current field of view~\cite{grogorick2017subtle,lange2020hivefive,gruenefeld2017eyesee360,marquardt2020guidance,binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear}, provides an established task in which to ask that question.

This study explores the impact of a self-similar agent voice, using search for an initially unnamed object in VR as the task. It evaluates how receiving spatial instructions in the SSV, compared with the SAV, affects task performance and the experience of doing the task. The comparison considers search performance and perceived workload, eye-tracking indicators of workload and search efficiency, and engagement, while keeping the task and guidance mechanism consistent across conditions. Because the voices can also differ in naturalness and other acoustic properties, resemblance is not the only difference between conditions.

Answering this question requires appropriate guidance in both conditions and a record of what each participant heard. Gaze contingency, the shared decision policy, and playback logging address this requirement. Interpreting the interaction also requires independent gaze-quality checks and separate records of looking, pointing, and selecting~\cite{dunn2024reporting,niehorster2026dataquality}. Chapter~\ref{ch:system} and Section~\ref{ch:measurement} describe these controls.

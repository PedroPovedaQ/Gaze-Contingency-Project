\chapter{DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS}
\label{ch:discussion}
\section{What the Study Can Explain}
The practical question is how to choose a voice for immersive assistance. Follow My Voice addresses one part of that decision: whether a synthetic voice resembling the listener changes search efficiency or experience relative to an already preferred alternative. This chapter sets out how the planned findings could answer that question, connect with prior voice research, and inform comparable applications. Because participant results remain pending, the interpretations are conditional rather than claims that either voice has proved better.

The discussion will distinguish three levels of evidence. RQ1 concerns measured efficiency; RQ2 concerns workload and motivation; RQ3 explores how participants describe and evaluate the voices and their reliance on guidance. Agreement among these outcomes could strengthen a design interpretation, while disagreement could reveal a useful tradeoff. Recorded speech and interaction provide the technical context for each interpretation, but cannot establish why a participant welcomed or ignored the voice.

\section{Interpreting Efficiency and Experience Together}
If self-similar voice produces faster active completion with acceptable uncertainty, the interpretation will still depend on the manipulation check and guidance quality. A difference accompanied by systematic intelligibility or delivery problems in the neutral condition could reflect those problems rather than anything specific to self-similarity. If both conditions have comparable delivery quality and participants recognize the clone, the result would more directly support a voice-condition effect in this task, although it would still not separate resemblance from other differences between the chosen and synthesized voices.

If the neutral voice produces faster completion, that would be consistent with a selected external voice being easier to follow, or with intelligibility or delivery problems in the cloned condition; delivery records and acoustic-quality reporting could help distinguish these readings, but the study would not establish the mechanism without further evidence. Liking, naturalness, and reliance ratings may help describe the experience. They should not be treated as proven mediators solely because they correlate with performance.

If completion times are similar, the width of the uncertainty interval becomes central. A wide interval would leave practically important differences unresolved, whereas a narrow interval near the null could constrain the size of a plausible effect under the tested conditions. A nonsignificant test alone will not establish equivalence. Equivalence testing is not part of the current analysis specification, and adding it would require a meaningful bound and procedure defined before inspecting the result.

Experience and performance may diverge. Participants may prefer their cloned voice while searching no faster, or may find it eerie while completing efficiently. Such outcomes would be substantively useful because they distinguish usability, affect, and speed. The battery is designed to retain these differences rather than force a single ranking.

Prior voice studies help explain why such a tradeoff would be informative. Kao et al. examine an avatar voice in a setting where continued voluntary play can reflect engagement, whereas Guo et al. report favorable impressions alongside eeriness in virtual collaboration~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar,guo2024doppelganger}. Here, a voice instructs the listener through a fixed search task. Faster completion therefore addresses efficiency, while enjoyment, discomfort, and debrief responses address the experience of following it. Differences across these studies would need to be interpreted in relation to the voice's role and the comparison condition, rather than treated automatically as agreement or contradiction.

\section{Practical Relevance and Transfer to Other Search Tasks}
The applications motivating the introduction identify where guided search could be useful, rather than domains already validated by this experiment. Locating task-relevant items in maintenance or training, finding a book, and noticing relevant VR content share a need to connect assistance with something in the environment~\cite{binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear,lange2020hivefive}. The present study isolates a voice-choice question within that interaction. It does not test industrial task completion, learning transfer, navigation safety, or narrative comprehension.

Its practical value will depend on the combination of outcomes. Faster search with acceptable workload and comfortable voice experience would support considering self-similar guidance for comparable tasks. Favorable experience without a detected speed difference could still motivate offering the clone as an optional voice, but would not justify claiming an efficiency benefit. Discomfort, intelligibility problems, or a preference for the neutral voice would instead identify limits to that option. These are conditional design implications to revisit after data collection, not recommendations established by the current draft.

Transfer also depends on how much information users already have. Here, the participant must use spatial guidance to infer an initially unnamed target; in Zhang et al.'s book task, the target title was supplied~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. A known-target task may allow people to continue independently and encounter the voice less often. Real work can additionally require recognizing meaningful parts, remembering procedural steps, moving through space, or coordinating with another person. Replication in those contexts would need to establish both the quality of guidance and the effect of voice choice. The current experiment supplies a controlled starting point for that work.

\section{Voice Identity and Acoustic Confounds}
Voice condition is more than a nominal label. The self-similar voice depends on recording quality and synthesis, whereas the neutral voice comes from a heterogeneous catalog, and the two may differ in pitch, prosody, speaking rate, pronunciation, and clip duration. Because the same words and provider do not guarantee matched acoustics, the study will document these properties and pilot comfortable intelligibility rather than claim exact equivalence.

Participant choice is intentional heterogeneity. Requiring every participant to use the same neutral voice would answer a different question. The analysis should retain chosen voice identifiers and describe the distribution, while avoiding an overfitted model with unsupported estimates for sparsely chosen voices. An exploratory association between a neutral voice and performance may reflect who selected it as well as its acoustic properties.

No extra pitch, panning, or inner-thought treatment is part of the current self-similar manipulation. Earlier attempts to make cloned speech sound like an internal monologue are development history. Reintroducing such an effect in only one condition would change the manipulation and require a revised rationale and analysis.

\section{Adaptive Guidance as a Shared Mechanism}
The audio-guidance literature supports the organization of the shared apparatus, while also constraining its interpretation. Gaze-dependent sonification, staged spatial cues, and audiovisual assistance investigate different ways to connect a cue with a search target~\cite{losing2014guiding,kwok2022twostep,binetti2021outofview}. Follow My Voice adapts these precedents into spoken orientation and refinement so that the assigned voice participates throughout the task. A difference between the two voice conditions would not demonstrate that speech outperforms those techniques, or that Jev improves search: neither comparison is part of the participant design.

A shared policy does not produce identical spoken sequences. Because participants turn, pause, look, and point differently, the policy supplies different directions and can emit different numbers of hints. This dependence on participant behavior is part of the design, not automatically a failure of experimental control.

Nevertheless, policy drift would threaten the comparison. Changing phrases, timers, or provider behavior midway through collection could alter assistance quality. The study release must pin the application build, configuration, phrase semantics, voice preparation settings, and decision-provider version where possible. Any unavoidable service change must be documented and considered in the analysis.

The central state machine improves auditability by giving one local owner to guidance evidence and transitions. It does not guarantee perfect guidance. Calibration error can produce an incorrect observation; an unclear reference frame can produce an unhelpful phrase; a stale response can escape an incomplete check. A defensible report describes the safeguards and measured failures rather than implying that architectural separation proves correctness.

\section{Practice, Learning, and Order}
One practice trial reduces setup burden but provides limited exposure to a complex interaction in which a participant must learn to hold gaze on the readiness cross, follow speech, use the chair comfortably, aim the right controller, and trigger selection. A fixed single trial does not prove that every participant has acquired these skills equally, and the practice-proficiency rule remains to be established through pilots.

Practice uses the first assigned voice, which aligns initial exposure with the first block but also couples practice familiarity with period. Counterbalancing helps distribute this coupling across voice conditions; it does not prevent fatigue, learning, novelty, or asymmetric carryover. These limitations will be reported with period and order effects and any relevant sensitivity analysis.

\section{Retrospective Questionnaires and Burden}
The final-only survey avoids interrupting the two-block search sequence with a long questionnaire, but it also requires participants to recall Block 1 after completing Block 2. Ratings may therefore reflect recency, contrast, or imperfect recall. Each rating set will identify its referenced block; the reminder linking that block to its voice remains to be piloted. These reminders may aid recall but cannot guarantee accurate recollection.

The condition-specific battery contains 130 numeric responses across both blocks, in addition to symptoms, setup ratings, comparison questions, and debrief. Completion time, missingness, and comprehension require pilot measurement. A participant who is tired or symptomatic should first receive the immediate post-exposure safety check. The survey's breadth is a current design decision, not evidence that every item is necessary or equally interpretable.

Adapting an embodied-agent measure to a voice agent can alter its meaning. Co-presence or anthropomorphism items may be understood differently without a visible character. The thesis will retain attribution and wording changes, report the adaptation, and avoid asserting new psychometric validation from a small experimental sample. Custom reliance and naturalness items remain individual exploratory measures.

\section{Gaze Quality and Feedback Coupling}
Gaze serves both as an input to assistance and as a recorded behavioral signal. Poor tracking can therefore change the participant's experience while also reducing measurement coverage. This coupling complicates interpretation: lower gaze availability is not merely a missing column if it also suspends guidance. Coverage and technical events must be considered alongside performance without automatically treating them as pre-existing participant traits.

A highlighted vicinity candidate can help indicate what the system currently attends to, but it can also suggest a reference object that differs from the mathematical gaze ray. For example, an instruction to look lower can be consistent with a gaze ray above the target yet confusing if the target appears higher than a highlighted neighbor; Appendix~\ref{app:guidance-support} describes this reference-frame failure. A cue should be evaluated in its visible interaction context, not solely against the sign of a geometric delta. Development-only rings and diagnostic markers must not silently enter the participant condition.

The proposed validity-aware stream and independent calibration checks address these risks, but headset-specific spatial performance and distinct acquisition rate remain empirical questions. The report must not borrow a Vive Pro Eye validation result and label it Focus Vision performance. Nor should it infer cognition from a convenient interaction threshold.

\section{Privacy, Consent, and Reproducibility}
A coded participant identifier does not make a voice recording anonymous. Natural and synthesized speech can remain recognizable. The consent and data-management documents must describe recording, synthesis, cloud processing, storage, access, and deletion using the services actually employed. Older local-only descriptions are inconsistent with current ElevenLabs voice preparation and Jev decisions and require reconciliation before participant use.

The thesis will report the relevant processing stages without including credentials or identifiable recordings. A reproducibility package can provide code, versioned schemas, example synthetic records, and de-identified summaries where authorized. Distribution of cloned voices or raw participant audio requires a separate basis and must not be assumed from consent to participate.

Cloud dependencies also affect replication. A future researcher may not obtain the same voice model or identical decision behavior. Preserving prepared-library metadata and actual playback records helps describe the original exposure, while a constrained phrase interface makes alternative providers testable. It does not eliminate the need to report provider changes.

\section{Future Work on Self-Similar Assistance}
Future studies could examine repeated exposure, task context, and the separate contributions of perceived resemblance and acoustic naturalness. Manipulating resemblance while controlling other acoustic properties would address a narrower causal question than the present comparison with a participant-selected neutral voice. These remain research directions, not conclusions from this study.

Other extensions could examine a visible agent body, a known-target search task, or a broader participant population. Each would change how the voice is encountered and would require a revised rationale and method. The current seated task supplies a specific context for evaluating self-similar assistance, not a universal claim about its value.

Memory, provider autonomy, and gaze-guidance policy could also be varied in separate systems studies. Those comparisons would concern the supporting mechanism and must not be inferred from the present voice contrast. Similarly, eye-event or pupil analyses should be added only when validated measurements answer a defined question about task demands or voice experience.

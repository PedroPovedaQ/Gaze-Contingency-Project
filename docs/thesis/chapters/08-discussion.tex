\chapter{DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS}
\label{ch:discussion}
\section{What the Study Can Explain}
Follow My Voice asks whether a synthetic voice resembling the listener changes search efficiency or experience relative to a neutral voice the listener has already chosen. Because participant results are pending, the interpretations below are conditional: they describe what the data could support, not which voice is better.

The discussion distinguishes three kinds of evidence. The primary question concerns measured efficiency, the secondary questions concern workload and motivation, and the exploratory questions examine participants' evaluations of the voices and their reported reliance on guidance. Agreement among these outcomes could strengthen a design interpretation, whereas disagreement could reveal a tradeoff that a single outcome would conceal.

\section{Interpreting Efficiency and Experience Together}
If completion time favors the self-similar voice, its interpretation depends on the estimate's uncertainty, the manipulation check, and guidance quality. A difference accompanied by systematic intelligibility or delivery problems in the neutral condition could reflect those problems rather than anything specific to self-similarity. If both conditions have comparable delivery quality and participants recognize the clone, the result would more directly support a voice-condition effect in this task, although resemblance would remain confounded with other differences between the chosen and synthesized voices.

Faster completion with the neutral voice would be consistent with a chosen external voice being easier to follow, but also with intelligibility or delivery problems in the cloned condition. Delivery records and acoustic-quality reporting could help distinguish these readings, although the study could not establish the mechanism without further evidence. Liking, naturalness, and reliance ratings may help characterize the experience in either case, but a correlation between such ratings and performance would not establish that they mediate the voice effect.

If completion times are similar, the width of the uncertainty interval becomes central. A wide interval would leave practically important differences unresolved, whereas a narrow interval near the null could constrain the size of a plausible effect under the tested conditions. A nonsignificant test does not establish equivalence. The analysis plan includes no equivalence test, which would require a meaningful bound and procedure specified before the result is examined.

A preference for self-similar speech may occur without a detectable improvement in completion time, while efficient search may coexist with discomfort.

Kao et al. examine an avatar voice in a setting where continued voluntary play can reflect engagement, whereas Guo et al. report favorable impressions alongside eeriness in virtual collaboration~\cite{kao2021selfsimilar,guo2024doppelganger}. Here, a voice instructs the listener through a fixed search task. Faster completion therefore addresses efficiency, while enjoyment, discomfort, and debrief responses address the experience of following it.

\section{Practical Relevance and Transfer to Other Search Tasks}
The applications that motivate this work indicate where guided search could be useful; this experiment does not validate guidance in those domains. Locating task-relevant items in maintenance or training, finding a book, and noticing relevant VR content share a need to connect assistance with something in the environment~\cite{binetti2021outofview,zhang2023seehear,lange2020hivefive}. The present study addresses a voice-choice question within that interaction and does not test industrial task completion, learning transfer, navigation safety, or narrative comprehension.

The practical value of the study depends on how its outcomes combine. Faster search accompanied by acceptable workload and a comfortable voice experience would support considering self-similar guidance for comparable tasks. A favorable experience without a detected speed difference could still motivate offering the clone as an optional voice, without supporting a claim that it makes search more efficient. Discomfort, intelligibility problems, or a preference for the neutral voice would instead mark limits to that option.

Transfer also depends on how much information users already have. Here, the participant must use spatial guidance to infer an initially unnamed target; in Zhang et al.'s book task, the target title was supplied~\cite{zhang2023seehear}. A known-target task may allow people to continue independently and encounter the voice less often. Real work can additionally require recognizing meaningful parts, remembering procedural steps, moving through space, or coordinating with another person. Replication in those contexts would need to establish both the quality of guidance and the effect of voice choice.

\section{Voice Identity and Acoustic Confounds}
The voice conditions may differ in acoustic properties as well as perceived resemblance. The self-similar voice depends on recording quality and synthesis, whereas the neutral voice comes from a heterogeneous catalog, and the two may differ in pitch, prosody, speaking rate, pronunciation, and clip duration. Because identical wording and a shared provider do not guarantee matched acoustics, the study documents these properties and the intelligibility observed in pilot sessions at comfortable playback levels, without assuming exact equivalence.

The variety of chosen neutral voices is a deliberate consequence of the design: requiring every participant to hear the same neutral voice would replace the comparison with a preferred alternative by a comparison with a fixed one. The analysis retains chosen voice identifiers and describes their distribution, but does not fit voice-specific estimates that sparsely chosen voices cannot support. An exploratory association between a neutral voice and performance may reflect who selected it as well as its acoustic properties.

The self-similar manipulation applies no additional pitch shift, panning, or inner-thought treatment. Processing that makes cloned speech sound like an internal monologue is excluded from the study; applying such an effect in only one condition would change the manipulation and require a revised rationale and analysis.

\section{Adaptive Guidance as a Shared Mechanism}
Gaze-dependent sonification, staged spatial cues, and audiovisual assistance investigate different ways to connect a cue with a search target~\cite{losing2014guiding,kwok2022twostep,binetti2021outofview,tanaka2026coarse}. Follow My Voice adapts these precedents into spoken orientation and refinement so that the assigned voice participates throughout the task. Because the participant design compares only voices, a difference between conditions would not show that speech outperforms those techniques or that Jev improves search.

A shared policy does not produce identical spoken sequences. Because participants turn, pause, look, and point differently, the policy supplies different directions and can emit different numbers of hints.

Policy drift, however, would threaten the comparison: changes to phrases, timers, or provider behavior during data collection could alter assistance quality. The study therefore fixes the application build, configuration, phrase semantics, voice preparation settings, and decision-provider version where possible, and any unavoidable service change will be documented and accounted for in the analysis.

Centralizing guidance evidence and transitions in a single local state machine makes guidance auditable but does not guarantee that it is correct. Calibration error can produce an incorrect observation, an unclear reference frame can produce an unhelpful phrase, and a stale response can pass an incomplete check.

\section{Practice, Learning, and Order}
One practice trial reduces setup burden but provides limited exposure to a complex interaction in which a participant must learn to hold gaze on the readiness cross, follow speech, use the chair comfortably, aim the right controller, and press the trigger to select. A single trial cannot ensure that all participants acquire these skills equally, and a practice-proficiency criterion will be established in pilot sessions.

Practice uses the first assigned voice, which aligns initial exposure with the first block but also couples practice familiarity with period. Counterbalancing helps distribute this coupling across voice conditions but does not prevent fatigue, learning, novelty, or asymmetric carryover, which are reported with period and order effects and any relevant sensitivity analysis.

\section{Retrospective Questionnaires and Burden}
The final-only survey avoids interrupting the two-block search sequence with a long questionnaire, but it also requires participants to recall Block 1 after completing Block 2. Ratings may therefore reflect recency, contrast, or imperfect recall. Each rating set identifies the block it refers to, and the reminder linking that block to its voice will be piloted. Such reminders may aid recall but cannot guarantee its accuracy.

The condition-specific battery contains 130 numeric responses across both blocks, in addition to setup ratings, comparison questions, and debrief. Pilot sessions will therefore measure its completion time, missingness, and comprehension. Because participants may be tired or uncomfortable after exposure, researchers check whether they are comfortable continuing before administering the retrospective sets.

Adapting an embodied-agent measure to a voice agent can alter its meaning. Co-presence or anthropomorphism items may be understood differently without a visible character. The thesis documents source attribution and wording changes, reports the adaptation, and makes no claim of new psychometric validation from a small experimental sample. Custom reliance and naturalness items remain individual exploratory measures.

\section{Gaze Quality and Feedback Coupling}
Gaze serves both as an input to assistance and as a recorded behavioral signal. Tracking gaps can change the participant's experience and reduce measurement coverage, affecting both the outcome being measured and the evidence available to interpret it. Coverage and technical events are therefore interpreted alongside performance rather than treated automatically as pre-existing participant characteristics.

A highlighted vicinity candidate can help indicate what the system currently attends to, but it can also suggest a reference object that differs from the mathematical gaze ray. For example, an instruction to look lower can be consistent with a gaze ray above the target yet confusing if the target appears higher than a highlighted neighbor. Cues are therefore evaluated in their visible interaction context, not solely against the sign of a geometric delta, and development-only rings and diagnostic markers will be excluded from the participant condition.

The proposed validity-aware stream and independent calibration checks address these risks, but headset-specific spatial performance and the rate of distinct gaze observations remain empirical questions. Validation results for the Vive Pro Eye cannot be assumed to hold for the Focus Vision, and an interaction threshold chosen for usability is not a validated measure of cognition.

\section{Privacy, Consent, and Reproducibility}
Coding participant identifiers does not make voice recordings anonymous, because both natural and synthesized speech can remain recognizable. Before participant use, the consent and data-management documents will be revised to describe recording, synthesis, cloud processing by ElevenLabs and Jev, storage, access, and deletion.

The thesis reports the relevant processing stages without including credentials or identifiable recordings. A reproducibility package can provide code, versioned schemas, example synthetic records, and de-identified summaries where authorized. Consent to participate does not extend to distributing cloned voices or raw participant audio, which would require a separate basis.

Cloud dependencies also affect replication. A future researcher may not obtain the same voice model or identical decision behavior. Preserving prepared-library metadata and actual playback records helps describe the original exposure, while a constrained phrase interface makes alternative providers testable, although provider changes must still be reported.

\section{Future Work on Self-Similar Assistance}
Future studies could examine repeated exposure, task context, and the separate contributions of perceived resemblance and acoustic naturalness. Manipulating resemblance while controlling other acoustic properties would address a narrower causal question than the present comparison with a participant-selected neutral voice.

Other extensions could examine a visible agent body, a known-target search task, or a broader participant population. Each would change how the voice is encountered and would require a revised rationale and method.

Memory, provider autonomy, and gaze-guidance policy could also be varied in separate systems studies. Those comparisons concern the supporting mechanism and cannot be inferred from the present voice contrast. Likewise, eye-event or pupil analyses would be warranted only where validated measurements can address a defined question about task demands or voice experience.

\subsection{Gaze-Informed Adaptation of Spoken Assistance}
Nerella et al.'s gaze-based prediction study also motivates testing whether momentary task demands can inform when an agent speaks~\cite{nerella2026gaze}. For Follow My Voice, such an extension would require task-specific labels, evaluation on unseen participants, and a separate test of adaptive hint timing. The cited work leaves evaluation of real-time deployment open, and its classification result does not show that adapting spoken guidance improves search.

\subsection{Complementing Behavioral Measures with Brain Signals}
A future extension could combine electroencephalography (EEG) with gaze, task performance, and questionnaires to investigate the attentional demands of receiving self-similar and neutral spoken guidance. Yang et al.'s \emph{Synchronizing with Attentional Sampling} provides a relevant precedent: the authors use EEG in immersive VR to investigate attentional sampling during gaze and hand--eye interaction as part of a study of visual flicker guidance~\cite{yang2026attentional}. This work motivates examining neural activity alongside observable search behavior, although its visual-guidance findings do not establish how voice resemblance affects attention or how frequently spoken hints should occur.

For the present voice comparison, synchronized recordings could relate neural activity to actual instruction onset, orientation toward the target, and controller selection. A follow-up study could then examine whether the two voices are associated with different responses during these stages, even when completion times are similar. Such measurements would complement the participant's reports of workload and experience rather than serve as direct measures of enjoyment, trust, or perceived voice similarity.

This extension would first require a feasibility study of EEG and headset compatibility, event synchronization, and the effects of eye movements, head turns, and muscle activity on the recordings. Neural measures and analysis windows would need to be specified in advance, with comparable task demands and speech exposure across voice conditions.

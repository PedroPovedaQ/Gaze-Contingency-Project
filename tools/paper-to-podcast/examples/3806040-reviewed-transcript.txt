Welcome to this research briefing on “Gaze-based Prediction of Cognitive Load in Augmented Reality,” by Greeshma Nerella, Daisy Gan, Brendan David-John, and Rawan Alghofaili. The paper appears in the 2026 Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, ETRA issue, with DOI 10.1145/3806040.

The central question is straightforward but consequential: can gaze data from an augmented-reality headset help distinguish moments when a user reports cognitive overload from moments when they do not? If so, an AR interface might eventually adapt the timing or amount of information it presents. But the details matter. This is not a paper showing that gaze is a direct, universal meter of mental workload. It is a study of one task, one headset, one operational label, and a set of exploratory models. That distinction is especially important when we consider what may transfer to the Vive Focus Vision Gaze Contingency study.

The authors ground the work in cognitive load theory, which separates intrinsic load, germane load, and extraneous load. Their focus is extraneous load: mental demand introduced by information that is not central to the user’s primary goal. In their study, interruptive notifications are the controlled source of that extra demand. The broader design ambition is a context-aware AR interface that can notice changing cognitive state and avoid delivering information at the wrong moment.

Thirty-one college and graduate students participated. Their ages ranged from eighteen to thirty, with a mean of twenty-one. Nineteen identified as women and twelve as men. All were screened for normal vision. Participants wore a Magic Leap 2, completed its standard eye-tracking calibration, and used a controller for selections. The system recorded gaze through the headset API at sixty hertz. The experiment was built in Unity 2022.3.11f1.

The study used a within-subject, dual-task design. Each participant completed five blocks. A block contained five minutes of the AR task, then a five-minute rest period and a NASA Task Load Index questionnaire. Including preparation and orientation, a session lasted roughly fifty to sixty minutes.

The primary task was a spatial color-matching game. Ten boards were arranged in a two-row by five-column arc spanning approximately minus ninety to plus ninety degrees around the participant. Each board held a three-by-three tile grid and an instruction such as “reveal three yellow tiles.” Some tiles were disabled, and participants had to reveal exactly the requested target-color tiles while avoiding extra revealed colors. When all ten boards were completed, a new set appeared. This arrangement required head and eye movement across a wide field.

The secondary task introduced interruptions. Alerts appeared at random intervals of about nine to ten seconds, at locations across a one-hundred-eighty-degree arc, sometimes inside and sometimes outside the current field of view. Each alert asked, “Did this alert increase your cognitive load?” Participants selected yes when they felt overloaded. They were told not to actively search for alerts.

This gives us two distinct workload records. NASA-TLX is a retrospective, block-level subjective workload instrument. The yes response to an alert is an event-level self-report used by the paper as the machine-learning label. Those are not interchangeable. The model’s positive class is not a clinical state and not a direct NASA-TLX threshold. It is a lens centered on a participant’s yes response to a particular interruption.

The data-processing pipeline begins with missing data. Segments longer than one second were removed. Gaps of one second or less were linearly interpolated. Gaze direction started in head-relative coordinates. The authors converted head quaternions to rotation matrices, rotated gaze vectors into world space, and normalized the resulting vectors. This step is essential in an immersive task because an eye direction expressed only relative to the head can confound eye movement with physical head turns.

The authors then extracted fifty-nine gaze-derived features. The main paper does not provide a line-by-line inventory of all fifty-nine, so we should not pretend it does. It does describe the feature families and names the ten selected features used in later analyses. Gaze velocity was computed from angular displacement between consecutive gaze vectors. A seven-sample median filter smoothed that velocity signal for event detection. The feature set included direct movement measures, dispersion measures, fixation behavior, saccade dynamics, durations, amplitudes, path lengths, velocities, and accelerations.

Saccades were detected with the Identification by Velocity Threshold algorithm, usually abbreviated I-VT. The reported velocity threshold was seventy degrees per second. In an I-VT detector, samples above a velocity threshold are treated as saccadic movement, while lower-velocity portions may contribute to fixation classification, depending on the implementation.

Fixations were detected with Identification by Dispersion Threshold, or I-DT. The paper reports a dispersion threshold of one degree per second, although dispersion is ordinarily expressed as an angular extent rather than a rate. That wording should be checked against the authors’ code or supplementary material before implementation. I-DT looks for a temporal group of gaze samples whose angular spread stays within a threshold. The paper argues that using velocity-based and dispersion-based event logic reduces dependence on a single detector, particularly because three-dimensional AR gaze is affected by head motion, scene dynamics, and noise.

That rationale is sensible, but it does not make the thresholds headset-independent. A seventy-degree-per-second velocity threshold and the reported one-degree dispersion setting are transferred methodological choices. They were not validated for the Vive Focus Vision, for our passthrough environment, or for our conjunction-search objects. Sampling behavior, calibration quality, binocular fusion, coordinate transforms, headset slippage, and task geometry can all change the distributions that those thresholds operate on.

After event detection, the authors derived fixation and saccade features. From the fifty-nine candidates they selected ten by testing, across time steps in the largest lens, which features most often showed significant differences between overload and non-overload. They state that selection occurred on training data only to avoid test leakage.

The ten reported selected features are: dispersion; gaze velocity; standard deviation of saccade velocity; mean saccade velocity; median saccade velocity; maximum saccade velocity; fixation path length; fixation duration; saccade path length; and standard deviation of saccade acceleration. Later XGBoost importance rankings emphasized movement-speed features, especially saccade velocity and acceleration, more than fixation features.

The temporal representation is also important. The paper calls each sliding window a lens. Lens sizes ranged from fifty to six hundred milliseconds, corresponding to three through thirty-six samples at sixty hertz. Windows were zero-padded at the edges. Rather than summarize a lens with a mean or another aggregate, the models received the temporal sequence of features within the window.

Labels were center-based. When a participant pressed yes on an alert, that time step was positive. A lens was positive only when that overload time step sat at its center; other lenses were labeled no overload. Centering can capture gaze patterns immediately before and after the response, but it also means the model may learn the motor, visual, or attentional signature of seeing and answering the alert. It does not by itself isolate a latent workload state.

Across thirty-one people, five blocks, five minutes per block, and sixty samples per second, the authors report two million seven hundred ninety thousand time steps, or forty-six thousand five hundred seconds. Positive events were rare. In the model-comparison dataset, they report one thousand five hundred seven positive training samples and more than two million three hundred sixty-eight thousand negative training samples before rebalancing. This extreme imbalance makes participant-level splitting and metric selection critical.

The first hypothesis was that gaze features differ between overloaded and non-overloaded moments. Feature trajectories were normalized per participant and compared across a six-hundred-millisecond context. The paper reports higher saccade-related values around overloaded events, interpreted as faster and more erratic movement. Fixation differences were subtle during the first five hundred milliseconds and increased toward the final one hundred milliseconds. The authors interpret this as reorientation or refocusing. These are group-level patterns around the operational event label, not a guarantee that the same pattern diagnoses workload for a new task.

The second analysis trained individual logistic-regression models. Four randomly chosen blocks from each participant were used for training and the remaining block for testing. Models using all ten features performed above chance across lens sizes, with the best all-feature result reported as an AUC-ROC of point seven four at six hundred milliseconds. Interestingly, gaze velocity alone reached point eight zero at six hundred milliseconds in the table, exceeding the all-feature individual model. Dispersion also improved with longer lenses. Many other individual features remained near chance.

The third analysis evaluated group models using leave-one-subject-out iterations. Training excluded one participant and testing used that unseen participant. The all-feature logistic models distinguished the classes above chance at every tested lens size. Single-feature group models also performed above chance, and gaze velocity alone was statistically comparable to all features at six hundred milliseconds in one reported comparison. Group models generally outperformed individual models, except that differences were not significant at two hundred and three hundred milliseconds.

The authors reasonably connect that advantage to data volume. An individual model had about one thousand four hundred fifty seconds of training data and five hundred seconds of test data. A group model had about forty-five thousand seconds for training and roughly one thousand five hundred seconds for the held-out participant. This does not prove that people lack useful individual patterns. It shows that a small personal dataset can underfit compared with a much larger multi-person dataset.

The final comparison fixed a five-hundred-fifty-millisecond lens and used the ten selected features. The data were split by participant before training and test extraction, so people did not overlap across the final train and test sets. The paper reports two million three hundred sixty-nine thousand eight hundred fifty training lenses and three hundred eighteen thousand two hundred sixteen test lenses.

Logistic regression, tuned with Optuna and five-fold cross-validation, achieved a validation AUC-ROC of point eight zero and a test AUC-ROC of point seven six. Random forest achieved point eight five in validation and point eight two on the test set. XGBoost achieved point eight eight in validation and point eight five on the test set, the paper’s best result. The summary table reports class-weighted precision, recall, and F-one values as well, though the exact relation between those weighted metrics and the confusion-matrix percentages deserves care when the classes are heavily imbalanced.

An AUC-ROC of point eight five means that, across thresholds, the model had useful ranking discrimination on the held-out test data. It does not mean that eighty-five percent of real-world workload events will be detected. Deployment requires a chosen threshold, calibration, sensitivity and specificity estimates, and costs for false positives and false negatives. In a gaze-contingent assistant, a false positive could suppress a helpful hint; a false negative could present a hint at a disruptive moment.

The limitations are substantive. First, notification frequency varied, but the study did not explicitly create sharply separated high- and low-load task conditions. NASA-TLX scores did not significantly correlate with the frequency of yes reports: the paper gives an r of minus point two and a p value greater than point five. The authors describe this as a gap between global retrospective judgment and event-level fluctuation. It can also be read as a warning that their positive label and the standard workload questionnaire did not converge in this design.

Second, individual learning curves were not informative because there were too few increments per person. Third, the work did not test real-time inference on the headset, so latency and runtime feasibility remain open. Fourth, participants were seated and performed one repeated task; ecological validity and cross-task generalization are uncertain. The paper itself notes that gaze models often transfer best between tasks with similar characteristics. Fifth, gaze data can reveal sensitive information, and a system that infers vulnerability could be used manipulatively. De-identification, transparency, access limits, and restrictions on adaptive use are therefore part of the research method, not an afterthought.

What, then, transfers to the Gaze Contingency Project?

Several methods transfer as strong candidates. We can transform Vive gaze directions into a consistent coordinate frame; explicitly record head pose alongside gaze; quantify missingness; avoid interpolating long gaps; compute angular velocity and dispersion; examine multiple temporal windows; split evaluation by participant; report class imbalance; compare simple baselines with nonlinear models; and analyze false-positive and false-negative costs. We can also preregister NASA-TLX, or another justified workload measure, as the primary subjective workload outcome while treating gaze features as exploratory.

The current Gaze Contingency runtime does not yet produce the same evidence. Its XRI gaze interactor casts a ray and generates hover-enter and hover-exit events when that ray intersects an object configured for gaze interaction. The logger records the gaze pose and hovered object metadata. Trial logic may also compute dwell on an object. Those are useful interaction proxies. They tell us that the platform’s interaction system considered a ray to be over a collider for some period.

But an object hover is not a validated fixation. A dwell is not automatically a fixation duration. Hover events depend on collider geometry, object size, scene layout, raycasting rules, sampling, filtering, and XRI state. They may remain active during smooth pursuit, head motion, or noisy gaze. Conversely, a true fixation could land on a location with no eligible collider and produce no hover. The Magic Leap study’s fixations and saccades were algorithmically detected from continuous gaze vectors after coordinate transformation, smoothing, missing-data handling, and thresholding. The two data products must remain explicitly distinct in our protocol and analysis.

For the Vive study, the appropriate next step is not to copy the paper’s seventy-degree I-VT threshold, its I-DT setting, its five-hundred-fifty-millisecond lens, or its XGBoost model and call them validated. The appropriate next step is to preserve high-quality raw gaze and head-pose streams, document device validity flags and sample timing, and run a local validation study. Threshold sensitivity should be evaluated across plausible settings. Event classifications should be compared with task events and, where possible, independently annotated or otherwise defensible ground truth. Reliability should be estimated on the actual Vive Focus Vision, in passthrough, with the bookshelf search geometry and participant movement used by this project.

The workload hierarchy should be equally clear. NASA-TLX is the primary established subjective workload instrument in this project unless the protocol specifies another validated measure. Trial performance, response time, errors, and hint interactions provide behavioral outcomes. Gaze velocity, dispersion, detected fixations, detected saccades, and hover dwell are exploratory signals until locally validated. A model score should not replace the primary workload measure merely because it is continuous or predicts the paper’s event labels.

There is also an experimental-design lesson. In the Nerella study, the alert that elicited a workload label was itself an interruption and a visual-motor event. In our study, a hint can likewise alter gaze, search strategy, and workload. If we use post-hint gaze to infer whether a hint was needed, cause and measurement can become entangled. Analysis windows should distinguish pre-hint state, hint presentation, orienting response, and post-hint recovery. Condition order, deterministic stimuli, and participant-level splits should be preserved so the model cannot learn round identity or object layout instead of cognitive state.

The paper’s most useful contribution is therefore a methodological blueprint and a feasibility signal. It shows that built-in headset gaze, processed carefully, can carry information related to event-level self-reports in a demanding AR task. It also shows why a large group dataset and participant-held-out evaluation matter. It does not validate a universal cognitive-load detector, a universal fixation threshold, or an off-the-shelf model for our headset.

For the Gaze Contingency Project, the responsible research position is precise: retain hover and dwell as implemented interaction proxies; add validated eye-event processing only as a separately tested analysis layer; use NASA-TLX as the primary subjective workload measure; keep gaze-derived workload features exploratory; and require local evidence before those features drive adaptive assistance. That is how this paper can accelerate the study without allowing an attractive AUC score to outrun the measurement validity beneath it.
